# Work - Play - Compete Part 1

## Summary

A philosophical piece built around a Steven Kotler line Trinsey found via Brian McCormick: "if we are hunting the highest version of ourselves, then we need to turn work into play and not the other way round." Trinsey separates three things coaches and athletes routinely conflate. Work produces an outcome. Play is done for its own sake regardless of outcome. Competition is a judged outcome, often a formalization of play.

The most useful passage is empirical rather than conceptual. Trinsey texted the Kotler quote to his 14 to 16 year-old team's group chat and every athlete read "play" as the thing you do on weekends against other teams, which is to say competition. He reads that as evidence of how far many teenagers sit from play in the sense Kotler meant, and notes an undercurrent of competition anxiety in athletes who are mostly top-5% in their age group and tracking toward college recruitment.

His own compressed version of the same idea is "Have fun. Get better. Have fun getting better," visualized as a pyramid where fun and improvement are the two bases supporting high performance.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: Work, Play, and Compete are three different things. Work is done to produce an outcome, play is done for its own sake irrespective of what it produces, and competition is a judged outcome. All three matter and each does a different job in development.
- Trinsey: his working slogan is "Have fun. Get better. Have fun getting better," framed as a pyramid where fun and improvement are the two bases and high performance sits on top.
- Trinsey: many kids have to Learn To Play, the same way they have to learn to learn. Play is more fun when you know the rules, and rules of thumb (the improv "Yes And" convention is his example) let shared play work for everyone involved.
- Trinsey: this is especially true for young teenagers, many of whom have had their play muscles either deconditioned or trained out of them over the previous half-decade. He notes that adolescent girls often go through a socialization phase where play shifts away from physical contests and toward social and emotional ones.
- Trinsey: when he sent the Kotler quote to his 14 to 16 year-old team, every athlete interpreted "play" as competition on the weekend, and the common answer was that working hard at practice makes competition go better and therefore more fun. He reads a hint of competition anxiety underneath a good sentiment.
- Trinsey: as a general rule, coaches should be more demanding in practice and more supportive in competition. He notes athletes at this level generally do not need to be pushed in competition.
- Trinsey: There Are No Blank Slates applies to work-and-play disposition. A playful coach can lose an athlete who arrives with a no-pain-no-gain attitude and hears "loosen up and have fun" as a lack of seriousness; a very serious coach can lose an improvisational athlete who feels she is being asked to overthink what she does naturally.
- Trinsey: a common cause of quitting a sport is that the work required for progress crowds out the play. He applies the same mechanism to professional retirement, which tends to arrive once improvement has stalled and the money is no longer life-changing.
- Trinsey: Deliberate Practice repeatedly knocks a learner out of flow, which is part of why it is hard. He is unsure how much of it is needed to get good at something, and thinks it becomes important mainly for getting great or for changing your natural development arc.

## Topics covered

- Work, play, and competition as distinct categories
- Flow and Deliberate Practice in tension
- Teaching adolescents to play, with a gendered socialization observation
- Competition anxiety in high-performing junior athletes
- Demanding in practice, supportive in competition
- Coach-athlete disposition mismatch on the work-play axis
- Attrition and retirement as work-play imbalance
