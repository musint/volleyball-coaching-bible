# Do Habits Exist Part 3

## Summary

Part 3 of three ([[trinsey-2026-do-habits-exist-part-1]], [[trinsey-2026-do-habits-exist-part-2]]) tackles the question a coach actually faces: an athlete does X, should you try to change it? Trinsey's frame is "good enough," and his answer is that the judgement has to be made over time rather than on any single play, because success in volleyball is being good over time rather than maximal on a given rep.

The connective idea is The Map Is Not The Territory. Trinsey notes that GMS clinics now present both Information Processing and Ecological Dynamics despite the two being theoretically incompatible, and says he does not mind, because what matters is whether a coach helps an athlete produce positive change. He extends the same skepticism to technique enthusiasts: the test of a technical intervention is whether it helped.

The passing material is the most concrete. Trinsey teaches beginners to wait with wrists and hands already connected, and gives his reasoning: platform formation is an unnatural human skill while running is a natural one, so the timing pressure should fall on the natural skill. He is candid that this is a minority position and that a common question at his youth-coaching seminars is how to stop kids running with their hands together.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: "how effective is the athlete at doing X" is the operative question, and the standard has to be calibrated to level. Phil Dalhausser was good enough to be the best in the world with a goofy-footed approach, and most seventh graders are not Phil Dalhausser.
- Trinsey: GMS clinics now teach both Information Processing and Ecological Dynamics, which is unsatisfying for someone who studies motor learning because the two theories are not really compatible. He accepts the tension because The Map Is Not The Territory: what matters is whether the map gets you where you want to go.
- Trinsey: games are won at the margins. The difference between hitting .250 and .300 is one more kill a week, and the difference between going 22-8 and 12-18 is 3 fewer errors per 100 sideout chances.
- Trinsey: he teaches beginners to wait with wrists and hands together and arms straight, because it makes a hands-together straight-arm movement solution more likely. His reasoning is that the unnatural elements of a sport need the most explicit instruction, forming a locked platform is unnatural while running is natural, and the hands have to connect on every play while most plays do not involve running.
- Trinsey: he dislikes the "up and out" passing prep common in women's volleyball and thinks it smacks of Fake Fundamentals, noting that passers who start wide almost always bring the arms into their center and back out anyway. He is explicit that this is a bias and that the play he uses as an example was passed accurately and sided out.
- Trinsey: There Are No Blank Slates. A passing prep posture did not emerge in a vacuum, is usually available to conscious report if you ask the athlete, and came from instruction, imitation, or the athlete's own experiment.
- Trinsey: the direct-instruction versus constraints-led debate under-weights the athlete's own thinking. A well-designed CLA game gets overridden if the athlete is privately coaching herself to "be low on defense" or "get my elbow up," and conversely explicit technical instruction fails when the environment discourages the technique.
- Trinsey: his four levers for influencing habits are bang for the buck (smallest intervention with the biggest effect, ideally prioritized by statistical analysis), keystone or starter habits, cognitive-overload management, and subconscious influence. He calls the quality of talent surrounding an athlete without a doubt a powerful environmental influence and maybe the most powerful, and notes the irony that EcoD-inclined coaches often feel uneasy about recruiting talent.

## Topics covered

- Deciding whether a movement solution is "good enough" to leave alone
- Map-versus-territory framing on competing motor-learning theories
- Margins arithmetic for hitting efficiency and sideout error rate
- Beginner passing prep: hands connected versus arms down and relaxed versus up and out
- Athlete self-instruction as an under-weighted variable in the CLA debate
- Four levers for influencing habit formation, including environmental and subconscious influence
- Deliberate Practice dosage and coach-side overuse of it
