# Incomplete Mastery Limits Transfer

## Summary

Trinsey flags this one as deliberately half-baked, and it is his most theoretically ambitious post in the batch. It braids three ideas: that most practice does not do anything, that competitive improvement is a treadmill where you have to outpace peers who are also improving, and that the Rote / Inflexible / Flexible knowledge distinction from education explains why some drill work fails to transfer.

The evidence anchor is Nebraska. Three athletes with three consecutive years of significant playing time, maximal program resources, and obvious motivation show flat service aces per set across those three years. Trinsey's reading is that most volleyball players stop improving much after a year or two at a given level of play, and that what actually stimulates change is a changing context.

The Inflexible-knowledge section is the coaching payoff. Trinsey argues that being able to execute a skill in an easy context but not a hard one is a necessary developmental step that cannot be skipped, and that an athlete with no capacity for the action at all will get worse when you add game context. His example is the scrape tip for middles: many cannot physically place the ball where it needs to go even in hitting lines, and dropping them into a 6v6 with a bonus for the shot will not fix that.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: Most Practice Doesn't Do Anything. Nebraska's three multi-year starters show flat aces per set across three years despite maximal resources, a strong staff, and motivated athletes doing extra work.
- Trinsey: most volleyball players do not improve much after a year or two of experience at a given level of play. Changing context is what stimulates change, which is why a 12 or 13 year-old who has only played bump-it-over ball improves sharply the first time she plays where three contacts happen.
- Trinsey: the game teaches the game, and some of what it teaches at U13 is false at U17. His examples: freeball to deep zone 1 at U13 versus the short 2/3 seam at U17; get a hand on the ball and hit a downball at U13 versus set the ball over to a good spot at U17; skip covering your hitter at U13 (the ball almost never gets blocked) versus cover carefully at U17 (the ball gets blocked more than it gets dug over).
- Trinsey: competitive improvement is a treadmill and a Red Queen problem. A backup setter has to improve faster than the starter, a team has to improve faster than the teams at the next tournament, and a small-market club has to improve fast enough to offset big-market clubs consolidating talent year over year (more families will drive an hour at U16s than at U13s).
- Trinsey: the 100% to 10% to 1% heuristic. To get a reliable 1% change in competition sideout you need roughly 10% underlying improvement, and to get 10% better at siding out you need roughly 100% improvement in whatever drill you are running. Concretely, go from winning Aceball 1 to 2 rounds out of 6 to winning 4 to 5 out of 6 before predicting with confidence that you will prevent aces at the next tournament.
- Trinsey: measured-change calibration from his own speed testing. A small test change might indicate nothing beyond test fuzziness or a better breakfast; a moderate, consistent change likely indicates real improvement in the underlying quality while remaining unlikely to affect competition; only a big change starts to affect performance. Outside observers volunteered comments about the athletes who had improved 0.2+ seconds in the 20m fly and 8 mph in max spike velocity.
- Trinsey: Rote, Inflexible, and Flexible knowledge map onto volleyball. Very basic blocked drills produce little to no reliable transfer, and so do tasks that are too hard. Inflexible knowledge, being able to perform a skill in an easy context but not a difficult one, is a step he does not think you can skip.
- Trinsey: his assessment sequence is to first ask for the skill in game context, since athletes often figure it out there. When the game context discourages the action the coach meant to encourage, he drops to easier reps, as easy as a coach toss. An athlete who cannot take a correct approach off a coach toss will rarely take the correct steps in a game, so he intervenes until the approach is in the ballpark off a toss while still letting her play 6v6 in the meantime.
- Trinsey: he flags a general problem with motor-learning research, that findings in one domain are assumed to generalize to others (a condition that works for golfers is assumed to work for basketball players), and says he rarely sees the assumption explored.

## Topics covered

- Why most practice fails to produce measurable improvement
- The competitive treadmill and talent consolidation across club seasons
- Context change as the primary driver of performance change
- What the game teaches at U13 that becomes false at U17
- Drill-to-competition transfer arithmetic (100% to 10% to 1%)
- Interpreting physical-testing changes as noise, real improvement, or competition-relevant
- Rote versus Inflexible versus Flexible knowledge applied to volleyball skill
- The scrape tip as a worked example of an underdeveloped shot
- Assessment sequencing from game context down to coach toss
