# Indoor Blocking Signals

## Summary

A Chris McGown email, posted to the GMS community board by staff, answering a
coach who described an elaborate blocking-signal system. McGown's reply is a
case for keeping the tactical menu short: at every level he coached, the team
carried three or four basic tactics outside the default read system, and the
signals existed to call those four things.

It is the cleanest available statement of the GMS blocking tactical vocabulary
(load, dedicate, commit, trap) with the specific hand signals attached, and it
pairs directly with the Load and Dedicate section of
[[gms-2025-learn-the-bunch-read-blocking-system]]. It also carries a useful
piece of communication design: McGown preferred pulling the libero or setter
over to relay information to the block rather than relying on signals at all.

The closing note is a philosophical one that constrains how much blocking
tactics can be expected to accomplish. McGown says he never worried much about
eliminating holes in the block, because blocking will always be a messy skill to
some degree, especially against a good or perfect pass.

## Key claims / ideas

- GMS (Chris McGown): at every level of play he coached, the team never had
  anything complicated. Three or four basic tactics existed outside the default
  system, which is read blocking with the wings helping on a quick in their zone.
- GMS (Chris McGown): the four tactics are Wings Load (do not help on the middle,
  just be great on the pin attacker), Middle Dedicate (a step toward an important
  attacker taken after the pass, toward a pin or another middle attacker), Middle
  Commit (jump with the opposing middle on a perfect pass), and Middle Trap (leave
  a pin hitter alone in order to double-team another pin hitter).
- GMS (Chris McGown): on a commit call the pins need to know, so they understand
  they are one-on-one, can signal 1 or 2 to the back row, and can play a line or
  angle bias while still reading the set and the hitter.
- GMS (Chris McGown): the hand signals were an L shown to the loading blocker, a
  point in the direction of the middle's dedicate, a closed fist for a
  read-commit, a T plus a point for a trap, and an open palm for stay and read.
- GMS (Chris McGown): trap and commit were rare in his men's teams. Trap was
  tried maybe once or twice a season in a highly specific circumstance, and commit
  came out only in certain circumstances against a few teams.
- GMS (Chris McGown): tactics were almost always part of the game plan and known
  beforehand, with signals as a supplement rather than the primary channel. His
  preference was to bring the libero or setter over to talk, then have them relay
  to the block.
- GMS (Chris McGown): he never worried much about trying to eliminate holes in
  the block, because the game is dynamic enough that blocking will always be messy
  to some degree, especially when the other team passes well.

## Topics covered

- Blocking tactical vocabulary: load, dedicate, commit, trap.
- Hand signals for in-match blocking calls.
- Pregame game planning versus in-rally signaling.
- Using the libero or setter as the communication relay to the block.
- How rarely trap and commit are actually used at a high level.
- Expectations about seams and holes in the block.
