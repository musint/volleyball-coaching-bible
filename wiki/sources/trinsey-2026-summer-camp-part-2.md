# Summer Camp Part 2

## Summary

The power and speed half of the camp write-up (see [[trinsey-2026-summer-camp-part-1]] for structure and philosophy). Trinsey explains why coaches conclude that speed is genetic, gives his measured season results across two years of sprint training with junior teams, and lays out exactly how he threads power work into practice without sacrificing court time.

The framing observation is that power development is real but slower than the noise around it. A 4-inch jump gain in a calendar year is excellent progress and works out to roughly 1/24 inch per practice, about the thickness of a credit card, so coaches can see no progress within a session or between sessions and reasonably conclude there is nothing to work on. His measured sprint results are more encouraging: a median improvement of just under 1 mph (about 6%) with a hand-timed U17 group one season, and 1.8 mph (about 13%) with a U16 group the next.

He is candid about a tension inside his own affiliation. At GMS clinics he tells coaches not to do power work at practice and simply to train volleyball at high intensity, because misapplied speed and strength work produces dead legs, shin splints, and sore shoulders. With more room to explain, he does power work with his own teams.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: power improvement is real and slower than the variance around it. A teenage athlete gaining 4 inches on her jump in a calendar year is fantastic progress, and at 8 practices a month that is about 1/24 inch per practice, roughly a credit card's thickness. Coaches see no within-session and no between-session progress, so they learn that speed is genetic.
- Trinsey: when he instructs at GMS clinics he tells coaches not to do power work at practice and just to train volleyball at high intensity, because misapplied speed, strength, and power training gives athletes dead legs, shin splints, and sore shoulders. He does power work with his own teams because he has room to explain it properly.
- Trinsey: he runs Tony Holler's Atomic Speed Workout from Feed The Cats basically exactly. The protocol is "10 in 10 and 2 in 6," meaning 10 speed drills in 10 minutes and 2 timed sprints with 5 minutes rest between.
- Trinsey: his measured results are a median just under 1 mph of sprint-velocity improvement (about 6%) with a hand-timed U17 group one season, and a median 1.8 mph (about 13%) with a U16 group the next. He explicitly declines to attribute the difference to method, noting the younger cohort has a steeper improvement curve.
- Trinsey: he abandoned electronic timing (Freelap) twice for club use as too slow and too finicky, and prefers hand-timing a fly or running an untimed race ladder. A consistent hand-timer with the same coach and the same watch gives a valid measure that matched the Freelap almost exactly, starting on the first foot strike into the fly zone and stopping on the first strike out.
- Trinsey: race ladders beat timed sprints on competitiveness, speed of administration, and equipment (a notebook and a circle around the winner), at the cost of losing the measurement athletes value seeing. He does not use "ready, set, go," preferring the coach to stand still and silent for a few seconds and then blow a whistle, so athletes do not learn to time the starter.
- Trinsey: power work goes at the start of practice, because power is best developed fresh and because building to two maximal sprints primes the nervous system for the competitive portion. His practice arc is Power, then a short technical portion, then the competitive team block, and he gives at least half of a standard 2-hour practice to the Teamwork portion, generally 6v6.
- Trinsey: he has a love-hate relationship with GMS-style serve-at-target work ("OTM"). He struggles to find great servers who never spent significant time serving at targets, and in a club team's limited practice time it does not feel like it justifies the bang for the buck. His resolution is to split the team into 3 or 4 groups and give each a 15-minute serve tutor every other week while the other groups do speed work, so everyone gets dedicated serving attention without the whole gym stopping.
- Trinsey: he adds "bounce" (reactive strength) to Holler's Sprint Fast, Lift Heavy, Jump High/Far framing and tests it with a Plyomat 10/5 RSI, two tests per practice with a minimum 1 minute and preferably 2+ minutes of rest. Sprint, Vertec, and RSI improvements correlated across athletes; spike velocity correlated moderately, with a few athletes getting faster and jumping higher without adding much to the swing.

## Topics covered

- Why power development is invisible on a practice-to-practice timescale
- Atomic Speed Workout protocol and its integration into volleyball practice
- Hand-timing versus electronic timing in a club environment
- Race-ladder format versus timed sprints
- Two-season sprint-velocity results with U16 and U17 teams
- Testing battery: 20m fly, Vertec spike touch, Plyomat 10/5 RSI, spike velocity
- Serve-tutor rotation as a way to fund isolated serving reps
- Itsy-Bitsy integration of physical testing alongside small-group games
- Practice-arc ordering: power, then technical, then competitive team block
