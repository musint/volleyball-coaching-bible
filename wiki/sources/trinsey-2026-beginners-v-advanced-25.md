# Beginners v Advanced 2.5

## Summary

The pace article in the Beginners v Advanced series (see [[trinsey-2026-beginners-v-advanced-part-2]] and [[trinsey-2026-beginners-v-advanced-3]]). Trinsey's thesis is counter-intuitive: beginners need a faster-paced practice than advanced players, and he decomposes pace into four independently adjustable variables so coaches can act on it.

The four are ball-entry speed, player-switch frequency, round duration, and how often the activity changes. Each moves in the same direction: faster and shorter for beginners, slower and longer for advanced athletes. His reasoning is a mix of physiology (advanced athletes expend far more per rally and need the recovery), safety (a rolling ball and a distracted teammate carry different risk at 12s than at pro level), and attention (fast entry with beginners is itself attention training, while between-rally time is productive for advanced athletes and mostly wasted on beginners).

The article closes on a teaching principle Trinsey borrows from writing instruction: athletes who struggle to execute one point well will not manage a competitive arc across a 25-point set, so meet them where they are and add one step at a time.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: practice pace is four things. How soon the next ball is entered after a rally ends, how quickly players switch in and out, how long a round of an activity lasts, and how frequently you switch activities.
- Trinsey: enter the next ball as soon as possible with beginners and allow more time between rallies with advanced players. Advanced athletes expend much more energy per rally (a 30-second rally between professionals leaves both teams fatigued), present real collision and ball-underfoot hazards, and use between-rally time productively.
- Trinsey: fast entry with beginners is attention training in its own right, building the reflex to look toward the action rather than chase butterflies. He is comfortable entering a ball at a 12s practice when one athlete is inattentive in a way he would not risk at a professional men's practice.
- Trinsey: rapid-switching formats (Speedball, Queens/Kings, Waves) suit beginners because the maximum time off the court is a couple of points. In his Mini-Volley format athletes rotate on every sideout with a maximum server run of 2 points. Keep a 12 year-old out of a drill for 6 consecutive minutes and expect boredom or tears.
- Trinsey: he keeps three uses for rapid switching with advanced players: warm-up (the jogging on and off helps), cutthroat or FBK-style elimination formats with roughly 3 athletes cycling, and training camp or first-practice-after-a-break sessions where he wants many combinations without high-intensity battles yet.
- Trinsey: shorter rounds suit beginners because their actions become more salient. Less mature athletes struggle to see how an action affects a distant outcome, and it is far easier to tell whether you won one point than to see how your actions change your odds across a 25-point set. Short rounds can feel too random to advanced competitors who understand there is always noise.
- Trinsey: for many high school aged players he likes rounds around 5 minutes; a 23-19 Aceball round runs about 6 minutes.
- Trinsey: beginners need "more" drills because they sustain attention for less time, and "more" is usually best delivered as small variations of the same activity every 1 to 2 minutes rather than genuinely new drills. He runs 4 to 5 activities in an hour with beginners while a more advanced team might do 4 drills in 2.5 hours. Constantly introducing new drills to beginners is actively bad.

## Topics covered

- Practice pace decomposed into four adjustable variables
- Ball-entry timing by athlete level, including the safety rationale
