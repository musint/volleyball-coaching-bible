# Radar Gun Serving

## Summary

Rob Neilson's GMS drill entry on using a radar gun to give servers precise
velocity feedback. It is short, and it carries one hard number that is hard to
find stated this plainly anywhere else: GMS's jump-float velocity sweet spot of
35 to 41 MPH.

The rationale is motor-learning rather than measurement for its own sake. Neilson
argues that speed and accuracy have to be trained together because they are linked
in the motor program, which means the athlete needs precise feedback about ball
velocity rather than only about landing location. A radar gun is the delivery
mechanism for that feedback.

The band is bounded on both sides for a specific physical reason. Below 35 MPH a
no-spin ball does not move enough and passers can track and respond to it; above
41 MPH the error rate becomes unacceptable. Dialing in an individual's best speed
inside that band takes work.

## Key claims / ideas

- GMS (Rob Neilson): speed and accuracy have to be trained at the same time,
  because the two elements are linked together in the motor program. This is the
  reason athletes need fairly precise feedback about ball velocity.
- GMS (Rob Neilson): jump float has a velocity sweet spot of 35 to 41 MPH at most
  levels, because a ball served with no spin moves the most at those speeds.
- GMS (Rob Neilson): below that band the serve is too easy, because passers can
  track and respond to the movement. Above it the ball is too hard to keep in the
  court and the error rate becomes unacceptably high.
- GMS (Rob Neilson): finding an individual athlete's best speed inside the band
  takes work, and a radar gun in practice supplies the immediate feedback that
  builds a feel for serving velocity.
- GMS (Rob Neilson): for jump spin, velocity is one of three factors alongside
  spin and a curving ball flight. The gun addresses velocity only, and players
  typically enjoy competing against their own previous speeds.
- GMS (Rob Neilson): the gun also creates pressure constraints in practice.
  Serves must exceed a set velocity to count, or to score points.

## Topics covered

- Jump float velocity band and why it is bounded on both sides.
- Speed and accuracy as coupled elements of the serving motor program.
- Radar as an augmented-feedback tool in practice.
- Velocity thresholds as a pressure constraint in serving games.
- Jump spin: velocity as one of three contributing factors.
