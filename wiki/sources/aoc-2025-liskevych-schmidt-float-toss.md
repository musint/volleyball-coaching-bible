# Working on the most important part of the basic float serve: The toss

## Summary

Two coaches split the toss problem between them. Terry Liskevych (USA Olympic coach 1984 to 1996, Oregon State 2005 to 2016) owns the accuracy half and prescribes an external target: place the ball on the same spot every time, with a sheet of paper on the floor as the physical target the toss must land on. His progression escalates to tossing with the eyes closed, which removes visual correction and forces the athlete to build the motion out of proprioception rather than mid-flight adjustment.

Gina Schmidt owns the mechanics and language half. Her central move is to rename the action: think "lift" rather than "toss," on the argument that the word itself invites less error and more control. Around that she gives three geometric checkpoints, a straight arm on the lift, release at 90 degrees, and alignment of the toss with the hitting shoulder.

The closing frame is an ownership argument. The toss is the one element of the match a server has complete control over, so it is the element worth the most isolated training time.

## Key claims / ideas

- Liskevych: a consistent float serve starts with the toss, and the operational standard is placing the ball on the same spot every time.
- Liskevych: use a physical floor target such as a sheet of paper so toss accuracy is externally scored rather than felt.
- Liskevych: tossing with the eyes closed is a progression step that builds consistency and body awareness by removing visual mid-flight correction.
- Schmidt: cue the motion as a "lift" instead of a "toss," which reduces errors and improves control.
- Schmidt: three mechanical checkpoints for the lift are a straight arm, release at 90 degrees, and alignment with the hitting shoulder.
- Both: the toss is the one skill a server has full control over in every match, which is the argument for training it in isolation.
