# Thought Provoking Passing Question

## Summary

A GMS athlete-modeling post taking a position against the "pick a side" passing
cue: the idea that a passer should decide as early as possible which side of the
body she will pass the ball on.

The argument is observational. GMS says it has discussed the idea at length with
national team and collegiate coaches and found them split, then reports that
teams which teach pick-a-side consistently pass the ball everywhere anyway, on
their sides and on their midlines. The accompanying clip shows a libero shuffling
right and taking the ball dead center on her midline.

The conclusion generalizes past this one cue: the demands of the game dictate
where passers contact the ball relative to their body, and with the right random
reps athletes solve the angle problem better than any coaching prescription does.
It pairs naturally with
[[gms-2025-should-players-move-before-contact]], which makes the same
anti-prescriptive argument about pre-contact movement.

## Key claims / ideas

- GMS: "pick a side" has been one of the trendy passing keys over the past few
  years, and national team and collegiate coaches are split on it. Some use it,
  others are not buying it.
- GMS: teams that teach the concept consistently pass the ball everywhere anyway,
  on their sides and on their midlines. The example given is a libero who shuffles
  to her right and takes the ball dead center on her midline.
- GMS: these passes "just happen" as passers do their best to create the right
  angles. The demands of the game dictate where passers contact the ball relative
  to their body.
- GMS: with the right kind of reps, specifically random reps, plus proper
  fundamentals, athletes learn to manage the angle variations passing requires
  better than coaching ideas can prescribe.
- GMS: declaring a team "anti-midline" or "we try to pass everything midline" is
  a complete waste of time, because clips of teams in both camps show the opposite
  behavior constantly.
- GMS: the coachable bottom line is that passers need to be good on their left,
  on their right, and on their midline, and coaches should stop thinking they can
  control which one happens.

## Topics covered

- The "pick a side" passing cue and the case against it.
- Midline versus off-body contact in serve receive.
- Random practice as the mechanism for solving contact-point variability.
- Limits of prescriptive coaching cues on self-organizing skills.
