# Winter 2026 Mailbag Part 1

## Summary

Three reader questions with extended answers. The first covers athletic blockers who swat at the ball with their arms coming back behind the head, and Trinsey's four-tool kit for teaching the press instead. The second is the most program-relevant: a first-year club coach with a national-level 12s team and an 11-player roster asks how to manage it, and Trinsey lays out an explicit rotation-by-rotation plan built on a 6-2 with no libero. The third addresses athletes who return to a high school program resistant to changing anything their club coach taught them.

The 12s answer is the load-bearing one for a youth program. Trinsey rules out the 5-1 at that age, recommends 6-2 or 6-3, and gives a named-athlete worked example that distributes playing time across setters, outsides, and middles.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: he would not run a 5-1 with a 12s team. His recommendation is a 6-2, or a 6-3 for coaches comfortable with it, chosen specifically so more athletes get playing time. A coach who does not know the 6-3 should wait a year and learn it in the off-season.
- Trinsey: the setter structure for a big 12s roster is 3 setters, 2 stronger and 1 weaker. The weaker setter plays every set but only one trip across the back row for one of the stronger setters, and a right-side hitter subs the other setter out of the front row.
- Trinsey: he highly recommends against starting a U12 season with a libero. Once an athlete puts on the jersey for a tournament, having to give it up reads as a demotion, and every athlete should get a chance to practice front-row as well as back-row. Let someone earn it in a later tournament.
- Trinsey: if a 12s libero is already in place, run two liberos. Play one more, and switch the second in at a fixed rotational point so the weaker athlete gets a reliable third of the rotations.
- Trinsey: swatting at the ball on the block is often worse in the best jumpers, because they hang in the air longer with nothing to do.
- Trinsey: his teaching tools for the press are blocking against a coach from the ground or a low box (a tall coach on a 30-inch box attacks at a height that encourages reaching up rather than across), Goldilocks cycles of intentionally two-moving then intentionally scraping the tape then almost scraping it, a side-along camera from a ref stand, and a pool noodle held 18 to 24 inches above the net as a ceiling the blocker has to finish under.
- Trinsey: he cautions against overdoing athlete self-video, because it risks pulling attention internal and over-focusing on micro movements at the expense of the vision and feel of the whole play.
- Trinsey: for athletes resistant to changing technique between coaches, he reframes rather than confronts. Professional athletes change systems and coaches constantly, and the goal of any technique is to be effective rather than to meet an arbitrary standard. His opening question to a resistant athlete is whether she is happy with the result, because an athlete satisfied with her outcomes will not change anything.
