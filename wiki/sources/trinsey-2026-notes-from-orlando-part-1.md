# Notes From Orlando Part 1

## Summary

Ten short observations written from AAU Nationals while coaching a 16 Premier team. The format is a grab bag, and the density of usable positions is high relative to the length.

Three items carry the most weight for a club program. The arm-development note extends the treadmill argument from [[trinsey-2026-incomplete-mastery-limits-transfer]] into a concrete seasonal target: by 16s Nationals the field is effectively playing like 17s teams, and an athlete who does not add 2 to 3 mph to her swing across the year will get dug more by defenders who improved. The defensive-crossing note is a stated change of position, Trinsey used to resist coordinating who goes in front and who goes behind, and after two club seasons of improvement he now plans to start there. The bench-coaching note is his highest bang-for-the-buck claim.

Two items are notable for cutting against his own affiliation. He reports payoff from coach-at-3 dig-set work while acknowledging it possibly defies GMS orthodoxy, and he flags that hitting warmups routinely undercut the offense a team trains, with every set on the tape and hitters raking the net.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: arm development is a tortoise-and-hare opportunity across a season. By 16s Nationals the teams are basically 17s teams, so some percentage of attacks that scored early in the season stop scoring against defenders who are 8 months better at digging hard-driven balls. An athlete who does not add 2 to 3 mph to her swing over the year gets dug more.
- Trinsey: when 16 year-olds see the block is up on them and the set is imperfect, the instinct is to take something off the swing, and that is precisely when they need to be more aggressive. Hitters willing to take a big rip at the hands usually get rewarded, and hitters who get less aggressive often end up blocked more because they cannot power it through.
- Trinsey: be explicit with athletes about roles, playing time, and expectations before a tournament, and lean slightly pessimistic rather than sugar-coating. His framing, from his father, is that people can stand to be disappointed and hate to be surprised, so a coach should let an athlete be pleasantly surprised instead.
- Trinsey: he used to resist coordinating defender crossing and dislikes defenses getting too scripted about it, since defense is less controlled than serve receive. After two straight club seasons of cross-court defense improving once he coordinated the middle-middle defender to cut in front and the cross-court defender to slide behind on the long angle, he plans to start there next season.
- Trinsey: coach-at-3 dig-set work has paid off this year, possibly defying GMS orthodoxy. Each rep is less game-like and a team still needs to dig a lot in live situations, and the volume of opportunities to practice extending to the ball in close proximity to a teammate is worth it, especially for drilling long-angle coverage coordination.
- Trinsey: club teams run roughly a 1:1 practice-to-competition time ratio, against roughly 3:1 for a college or professional team. That makes tournament warmups worth treating as training time, giving a couple of athletes something specific to focus on rather than delivering a lot of feedback.
- Trinsey: Teaching The Bench is one of the best bang-for-the-buck coaching moves available. The next athlete to face a situation may be on the bench and coming in a rotation or two, and a bench conversation allows 15 seconds and a two-way exchange with a question, where an on-court athlete can only get a quick shout.
- Trinsey: hitting warmups undercut the offense teams train. Coaches emphasize keeping sets off the net all season and then run warmups where every set is on the tape and hitters rake the net. He is considering putting blockers into hitting warmups, having never done it before.
