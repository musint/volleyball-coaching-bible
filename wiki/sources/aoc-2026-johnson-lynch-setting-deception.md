# Setting deception: Be tricky? Or be consistent?

## Summary

Christy Johnson-Lynch (Iowa State) resolves the deception-versus-consistency question by ranking them rather than choosing between them. Consistency is the benchmark of a setter's game and the thing she is measured on. Deception is a periodic overlay that keeps blockers honest, and it earns its value precisely because the baseline is consistent.

The mechanical core of her deception argument is postural rather than tactical. The setter holds a neutral stance whether she is setting front to the outside or back to the right side, and her hands are neutral and above the head before the ball arrives. Deception here is the absence of a tell, and it costs nothing because it is the same posture she would hold anyway.

The tactical overlay is occasional and situational: a set pushed to the opposite pin on a tough pass, or a dump into the middle of the court. Johnson-Lynch's framing is that these keep blockers on their toes and give the offense an edge, layered on top of a game whose benchmark remains consistency.

## Key claims / ideas

- Johnson-Lynch: the setter's neutral stance must be identical whether she is setting behind to the right side or in front to the outside, so her body gives away nothing.
- Johnson-Lynch: the setter's hands should be neutral and above the head before the ball reaches them, and showing the set is the thing to avoid.
- Johnson-Lynch: consistency is the recognized key to becoming a good setter and remains the benchmark of the position.
- Johnson-Lynch: a setter also needs savvy, delivering occasional surprises such as a set pushed to the opposite pin on a tough pass or a dump into the middle of the court.
- Johnson-Lynch: the value of deception is keeping blockers on their toes, which produces a team-level edge on top of a consistent baseline.
- The article frames setting deception as a mental-game topic for the setter position rather than a mechanics topic.
