# Summer School 2026

## Summary

Trinsey's statement of his own coaching canon and the framework he organizes his writing around. He names three books, Nick Winkleman's *The Language Of Coaching*, Rob Gray's *How We Learn To Move*, and Rollnick and Fader's *Coaching Athletes To Be Their Best*, and maps each to one of three coaching sub-roles: Drill Designer, Tutor, and Mentor. Systems are the "what," statistical understanding of the game is the "why," and these three sub-roles are the "how."

Each section carries a compressed one-paragraph summary of how Trinsey actually applies the book, and those summaries are the most citable material on the page. The Winkleman summary in particular ("intent is the most important influence on the outcome of a play; attention is the currency of coaching") is the position he expands in [[trinsey-2026-good-intentions]].

The Drill Designer section contains a claim worth arguing with: the baseline any drill has to beat is pure scrimmage, which Trinsey calls a productive activity that could make a coach above average on its own. His framing is that drill design does not matter until it does, and its main function is avoiding activities less productive than scrimmaging.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: his three-book coaching canon is Winkleman's *The Language Of Coaching*, Gray's *How We Learn To Move*, and Rollnick and Fader's *Coaching Athletes To Be Their Best*, mapped to Drill Designer, Tutor, and Mentor. Systems are the what, statistics are the why, and these three sub-roles are the how.
- Trinsey: Practice Planning is not a lifelong skill for most coaches. Seasons require planning up front to lay out a template and season blocks, after which running the same template repeatedly is fine.
- Trinsey: Drill Design is a different skill and is totally unimportant until it is. The baseline to beat is pure scrimmage, which is a productive activity, and a coach whose only drill was scrimmage could be above average in rate of improvement. The risk of elaborate drill design is doing things at practice that are less productive than scrimmaging.
- Trinsey: his summary of the ecological frame is a 3-way interaction between the player, the task, and the environment, where the player's self-perception both influences how she attempts a task and is influenced by task and environment. His plain-language translations: two players swing differently at the same set; the same player responds differently to the same instruction depending on mood; the same set gets swung at differently depending on how the block looks; and the same swing feels different at 0-0, 15-15, and 24-24.
- Trinsey: because the situations matter more than the format, whether you run 3v3 or 4v4 on a given day mostly does not matter. This is also why he repeats the same practice templates and the same drills, so he learns how specific athletes interact with each game.
- Trinsey: his summary of Winkleman is that an athlete's intent is the most important influence on the outcome of a movement, coaches should prioritize influencing intent and constantly check for understanding, and attention is the currency of coaching.
- Trinsey: his cueing model is Intention to Attention to Power to Energy. Many elite players think about the game in a much simpler way than mediocre players, and coaches compound the problem by overcomplicating things in a desire to teach faster.
- Trinsey: his summary of Motivational Interviewing is that direct intervention backfires when athletes are emotional, that rapport built by identifying and reflecting feelings precedes influence, and that connecting behaviors to an athlete's own values often produces the athlete expressing a desire for the new behavior, at which point direct instruction works. He explicitly carves out the unemotional case: for a behavioral request the athlete has no emotional tie to, direct and clear instruction is often best.
- Trinsey: behavior that looks like a motivation problem is often a skill problem. Athletes who let balls drop without diving are usually not bad at caring; they are usually bad at diving, so it hurts, and it hurts more to dive and miss.
