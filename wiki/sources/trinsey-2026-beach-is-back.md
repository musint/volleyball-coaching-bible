# Beach Is Back!

## Summary

A clip-by-clip breakdown of ten block and defense sequences from the Outrigger, an early-season NCAA beach tournament, drawn from Trinsey's long-running statistical consulting work with the LMU beach program. The stated purpose is training his own club's beach athletes on defensive eyework and on coordinating blocker stay-or-pull decisions with the back-court defender.

**Scope note:** beach volleyball is out of scope for this wiki per SCHEMA section 1. This source page exists for completeness of the Smarter Volley ingest and because the read content, blocker set-recognition, stay-versus-pull, hit-block versus shot-block, and on-2 awareness, is the same perceptual skill family the indoor pages treat. Treat the beach-specific tactical material as reference rather than as program guidance.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: blocker recognition of the set is the pivotal read. The micro-clues are the setter squaring slightly off the net and the set then flaring off the net, which together indicate "pull."
- Trinsey: good readers do not process those clues consciously. The cues are legible on video after the fact even though the athlete is not deliberating over them in real time.
- Trinsey: at more advanced levels blockers add a hit-block versus shot-block read, going high with the block when they see the hitter slow down and come inside the ball to shoot high line.
- Trinsey: defenders cannot fixate on the hitter too early, because the setter can attack on the second contact, and the blocker and defender then need a coordinated answer for who takes the cut-back and who runs across.
- Trinsey: most hitters try to beat the puller, so the most common ball when the blocker pulls off is heat toward the center of the court. The defender's job is to sit in her read rather than break for the shot.
- Trinsey: going behind is often valuable on defense, because most hitters are better at running their route and hitting behind than defenders are at adjusting their defensive lineups.
- Trinsey: two-blocks are less common than one-calls because they are hard for many teams to line up without giving the call away early and conceding an easy cross-court roller.
- Trinsey: pressure on blocker eyework compounds when the blocker is also the server running up, when the offense is going behind, and when the set is kept low.

## Topics covered

- Beach block and defense eyework
