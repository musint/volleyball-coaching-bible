# Beginners v Advanced 3

## Summary

The noise article in the Beginners v Advanced series (see [[trinsey-2026-beginners-v-advanced-part-2]] and [[trinsey-2026-beginners-v-advanced-25]]), written from the NCAA Beach Championships in Gulf Shores. Trinsey reframes "good technique" in signal-and-noise terms: what coaches usually mean by clean or fundamentally sound is that the athlete's motion is directed at the goal, with little extra unrelated movement.

From there he applies Differential Learning, crediting Wolfgang Schöllhorn as the pioneer of transferring the signal-boosting-noise idea from information theory into movement coaching. Trinsey is careful to flag that his own Goldilocks Method uses scripted deviations rather than the truly random noise Schöllhorn's system relies on, and says Schöllhorn might not sanction it as true Differential Learning.

The practical payoff is a level-calibrated rule: **variability happens to beginners and is used by advanced players**. Because beginners already carry high accidental variability, adding more risks the over-noised condition where the signal is obscured, so with beginners Trinsey works to reduce noise, and only with advanced athletes does he inject it. He gives five reduction levers and three addition levers.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: when coaches say "clean," "fundamentally sound," or "repeatable," what they usually mean is low noise, movement directed at the goal rather than biomechanical perfection. Most decent coaches already accept that there are several ways to skin a cat.
- Trinsey: Differential Learning holds that adding an appropriate amount of random noise to a faint signal strengthens it, while too much obscures it. He credits Schöllhorn as the pioneer and flags that his own Goldilocks Method uses scripted deviations rather than truly random noise.
- Trinsey: "variability happens to beginners and is used by advanced players." A beginning setter is surprised by her own variability; an elite setter introduces it deliberately, leaving a set slightly inside because the outside hitter had to move into the 5/6 seam, or hanging a transition set higher off the net.
- Trinsey: because beginners already carry high accidental variability, adding more risks the over-noised condition. Much of coaching beginners is sharpening their understanding of what actually just happened. His mental model is instant-replay resolution: advanced athletes have crystal-clear 4K replay in their heads, beginners have a grainy black and white system.
- Trinsey: five levers for reducing noise for beginners. Play more 1v1 and 2v2 (6v6 introduces so many interactions that beginners cannot sift out their own contribution); use more single-contact and 2-contact drills; put a calmer, simpler, more skilled "coach" into the 3-contact sequence, ideally an older athlete rather than a staff member; keep entries simple and predictable; and use either/or tasks as a bridge into variability (attack an in-system ball then an out-of-system high ball, serve deep then short).
- Trinsey: 1v1 and 2v2 remain valuable all the way to elite level, though at that level they become general preparation and warm-up rather than specific high-transfer drills. For beginners they are gold.
- Trinsey: three levers for adding noise for advanced players. Swap heavily blocked repetitious tutoring for Goldilocks-style variations; put athletes in disadvantageous situations (passers standing a full step deeper or shorter than they prefer, so they deal with different reception timings); and vary the inputs or second-next ball frequently and unpredictably.
- Trinsey: the LMU differential serving example holds the outcome constant (a clean float) while scripting the deviation: toss too far forward, toss slightly behind, jump off two feet then one then the other, toss left-handed for a hybrid spin. For an appropriately advanced athlete, dealing with that noise while still producing the clean float teaches the float better at a mostly subconscious level than repeating identical reps.
- Trinsey: advanced teams have limited in-season practice time relative to their tactical demands, and at elite level systems Organization is both more important and more realistically improvable in a short window than general team skill level. Technical-noise drills like differential serving are therefore useful as upgrades to existing tutor activities rather than as replacements for systems work.

## Topics covered

- Signal and noise as a working definition of technique quality
- Differential Learning and the Goldilocks Method, with the departure from Schöllhorn flagged
- Level-calibrated noise: reduce for beginners, add for advanced athletes
- Small-sided formats (1v1, 2v2) and their changing role by level
- Coach-in-the-sequence as a noise-reduction device
- Differential serving protocol from an NCAA beach program
- Systems Organization versus general skill as the in-season improvement lever at elite level
