# Summer Camp Part 1

## Summary

The structural half of Trinsey's camp write-up (see [[trinsey-2026-summer-camp-part-2]] for the power and speed applications). He gives the full schedule for a U12 camp, 2 days, 9:00am to 3:00pm, roughly 14 athletes per court, three 90-minute sessions, and then defends the design choice that shapes everything else: this was not an all-skills camp.

His case against the all-skills format has four parts, and the two about technical training are the ones with the most reach into age-guide territory. Young athletes need less technical training rather than more, first because most have not built any habits yet to correct, and second because prepubescent proportions mean technical work on hand shape or platform has little claim on the body they will have as teenagers. He names the exceptions he does teach young: how to stand, toss, and step into a serve, and how to take an approach and jump off two feet.

The Power / Ball Control / Teamwork framework introduced here is his team-profiling model and structures every session. The camp's opening 20-minute ball-control block is described in unusual detail, and the point of it is not passing technique at all: it installs performance goals, success criteria, verbalized counting, self-refereeing, partner switching, and laddered competition.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: the camp structure is 2 days, 9:00am to 3:00pm, about 14 athletes per court, with three 90-minute sessions split by a 15-minute snack break and a 75-minute lunch. Session 1 ran roughly 20 minutes ball control, 25 minutes power, 45 minutes teamwork.
- Trinsey: he rejects the all-skills camp format (X minutes on passing, then Y on setting, then Z on approach) as boring and context-stripping. Athletes taught to pass in the morning do nothing related to it when they eventually play, and camp design should at least avoid guaranteeing that practice will not transfer from morning to afternoon.
- Trinsey: young athletes need less technical training rather than more, for two reasons. Most have not built the sub-optimal habits that technical training exists to correct, and prepubescent bodies are proportioned far enough from their adult form that technique work on hand shape or platform has little transfer to teenage habits. A 4'10" athlete building a habit that guarantees a technically perfect platform at 5'10" does not correspond to how the body works.
- Trinsey: the technical training he does teach young athletes is how to stand, toss, and step into a serve, and how to take an approach and jump off two feet.
- Trinsey: it is not especially difficult to teach technique to athletes who are engaged, motivated, athletic, and responsive to the game, and it is very difficult to work with athletes who are unengaged. In club volleyball the level you can enter (Open, USA, Club) is determined by physicality and your success within that level is determined by skill, with overlap.
- Trinsey: applied athleticism requires a stimulus that demands it. Few young athletes will naturally set a ball at attackable height in partner setting, and almost none will apply maximum power to push it. The coaching urge to "set the ball higher" works, and putting the athlete in a competitive situation that forces a powerful set is a better stimulus. His staple game for this is Tug-of-War with the front court out of bounds, so every push must be deep.
- Trinsey: his team-profiling framework is Power, Ball Control, and Teamwork, and he applies all three to every session. In a normal team practice he starts with Power because his speed and spiking work doubles as warm-up; at camp he starts with ball control so athletes can loosen up and build toward maximal effort, since starting a U12 camp with marching and skipping will not produce what you want.
- Trinsey: the opening partner-passing block is not about passing. It installs performance goals (most in a row), explicit success criteria (partners on the 10-foot line and the baseline so each pass travels 20 feet), verbalized counting as an onramp for quiet athletes who otherwise never practice using their voice, self-refereeing capacity so competitive mini-games can run, partner switching, and laddered pair-versus-pair competition.
- Trinsey: the Competitive Dichotomy is that you have to care enough to do everything in your power to win every point while accepting that you sometimes will not, and that only a game you risked losing is worth winning. Applied to practice, it means trying your hardest to beat teammates because that is what lets them rise, then switching sides five minutes later.
