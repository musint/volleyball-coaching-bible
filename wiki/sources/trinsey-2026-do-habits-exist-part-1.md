# Do Habits Exist? Part 1

## Summary

Part 1 of a three-part series ([[trinsey-2026-do-habits-exist-part-2]], [[trinsey-2026-do-habits-exist-part-3]]) in which Trinsey works out where he lands between the motor-program account of skill and the ecological-dynamics account. His answer is that habits do exist in the colloquial sense and that the ecological reading of them as attractor wells rather than stored motor files is compatible with that. What he rejects is the coaching usefulness of habit language for in-play dynamic actions.

The argument runs through perception-action coupling, credited to Rob Gray, and a worked case study of one of his setters. Video of every Setter-5 and Setter-4 release from her first two matches in December is compared against the same selection from the Sunshine Qualifier in February. The improvements Trinsey names, consistent clockwise turn, releases that clearly cross past the opposing middle blocker, and a lead-leg finish, are all downstream of arriving in a position from which a lead-leg finish was available. His point is that the setter always knew how to finish left-right on a lead leg; her body would not select that solution when the ball was behind her.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: habits do exist in the colloquial sense. Most people in the ecological-dynamics sphere would grant that certain invariants define a class of movement and that athletes have attractor wells producing consistent output across varied environmental interactions.
- Trinsey: describing dynamic in-play actions in habit terms is somewhere between unhelpful and counterproductive for coaching, because the action was selected by what the athlete perceived. "Get your elbow up" fails on a late hitter, since reaching high is why she would whiff, and the dropped elbow is the solution rather than the error.
- Trinsey: trying to consciously override what the body has perceived as the best course of action is as likely to hinder an athlete as help her.
- Trinsey: perception and action are coupled, an insight he credits to Rob Gray. When teaching new ways to move around the court he starts with what the athlete will see before and during the movement, on the reasoning that improving perceptual quality almost always improves movement quality.
- Trinsey: to the extent coaches see results from split-step work, he suspects the mechanism is getting players to attend more closely to server contact rather than the split step itself, which he thinks is usually best left to the body to time.
- Trinsey: the four applied consequences of perception-action coupling are that physically uncomfortable players learn poorly, cognitively overloaded players move worse, changing what athletes look at changes how they move, and changing body posture changes what they see.
- Trinsey: the operational reframe he takes from Duhigg's *The Power of Habit* is "you don't change habits, you create new ones," which he says to athletes who describe having a bad habit of doing something.
- Trinsey: in the setter case study the December footage shows both directions of turn, no release that clearly crosses the midpoint, and several plays he calls a "no release" where she wanders toward the passing target and bump-sets from wherever the ball took her. In February she turns clockwise every time and gets across in all but one clip, which is what makes an actual setting decision possible.
