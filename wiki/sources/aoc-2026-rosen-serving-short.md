# Disrupt the opponent by serving short

## Summary

Mark Rosen (Michigan 1999 to 2022) presents the short serve as a tempo weapon aimed at the opposing offense rather than at the passer. The mechanism he describes is second-order: the short serve rushes the pass, and the rushed pass forces hitters who were already moving into their approach to stop, reset, and adapt. That adaptation costs the opponent time, and the time it costs slows their attack tempo enough for the serving team's block to form and set up a stuff.

The framing matters because it separates two things coaches often bundle. A short serve is not primarily an ace-hunting serve or a passer-error serve. It is a serve whose value shows up one contact later, in the quality of the block matchup your team gets. Rosen's prescription is to rep short serves in practice specifically so coaches can observe the change in the opponent's offensive flow.

## Key claims / ideas

- Rosen: the short serve's purpose is to slow the opposing offense down, which buys the serving team time to set up a stuff block.
- Rosen: a short serve rushes the pass, and hitters are "taken aback" and must adapt mid-approach; that adaptation time is what slows the offense.
- The payoff of a short serve is measured at the block, one contact after the serve, rather than at the pass.
- Short serving is a trainable, deliberately repped tactic, and coaches should run it in practice to observe its effect on opponent offensive flow.
