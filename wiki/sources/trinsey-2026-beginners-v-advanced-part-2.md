# Beginners v Advanced Part 2

## Summary

Part 2 of the Beginners v Advanced series (see also [[trinsey-2026-beginners-v-advanced-25]] and [[trinsey-2026-beginners-v-advanced-3]]; Part 1, on court size and net height, is not in this ingest). The whole article is about how practice starts, and its central observation is counter-intuitive: professional practices start far more slowly than club practices, which inverts the trend a coach would extrapolate from watching good and bad high school teams.

Trinsey attributes the difference to structure rather than to standards. Professional teams have effectively unlimited practice time and no hard in or out, so athletes trickle onto the court in ones and twos, do individualized preparation, and hit a formal start once everyone is already warm. He names this a **soft start** and contrasts it with the **hard start** most club teams face, where you are waiting for another group to clear the court. The prescription is to know which one you have and design accordingly.

The Mini-Volley soft-start walkthrough (4:30 through 5:00 pm, minute by minute) is the most reproducible part of the piece, and Trinsey names four reasons the informal pre-practice time is valuable rather than wasted.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: professional and national-team practices start much more slowly than club practices. The natural extrapolation from "good high school teams practice faster than disorganized ones" does not continue upward.
- Trinsey: club coaching is an exercise in maximizing practice time for three reasons. Athletes with less volleyball background change more within a season than a professional entering with 10+ years of experience; athletes are physically maturing and will be about 6% older by season's end, so the training stimulus shapes their adaptation; and there is simply not enough practice time.
- Trinsey: with a hard start, have a very clear way that practice begins. His elements are meeting at the board (players need to know why, and immature athletes operate on a short time horizon and can struggle to plan even across one practice), a high-five ritual requiring Eyes, Hands, and Names, then either warm-up into physical training, a 1-touch competitive game, or skills tutoring.
- Trinsey: he judges every dynamic warmup against simply jogging a few laps, which he thinks has become underrated by enlightened coaches. The operative criterion for being warm is sweating or near-sweating. At U12s he is comfortable starting straight into playing volleyball; with professionals he is not.
- Trinsey: serving and setting tutoring are good practice openers because they do not require the athlete to be very warmed up. His baseline evaluation for a practice start is "jog 3 laps and then start playing 3v3," and he notes many practices start slower and less effectively than that.
- Trinsey: a soft start is a gift even though it looks less organized. The four elements that make the informal pre-practice window valuable are autonomy and control, mixed-age and mixed-level interaction, playing with more mature players (a 16 year-old's ball control produces better and longer play out of a 9 year-old), and younger athletes glimpsing older athletes being coached in a focused session.
- Trinsey: he explicitly warns against being the coach who says nobody can get a ball out until everyone is ready to start, and frames the pre-practice window as a lever on autonomy, coach attention, fun, and good habits.
- Trinsey: he does not like starting practice with classroom sessions, because athletes have been sitting still in school all day and the analytic thinking a classroom session demands is the opposite of the sensory processing good volleyball performance needs. He minimizes the damage with a 2-20-120 method, sending a short video beforehand so the discussion portion can be active and brief, and his ideal version skips the sit-down entirely.

## Topics covered

- Hard starts versus soft starts and how to design for each
- Why club practice time carries more marginal value than professional practice time
- Warm-up standards and the sweat criterion, by age
- Ritual openers (meet at the board, Eyes / Hands / Names high-fives)
- Combining speed work, tutoring, and small-sided play in the opening block
- Mini-Volley soft-start structure for ages 6 to 11
- Classroom sessions and the 2-20-120 method
