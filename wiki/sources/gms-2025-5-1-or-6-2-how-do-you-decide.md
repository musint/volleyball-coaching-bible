# 5-1 or 6-2? How do you decide?

## Summary

A long-form discussion-board post by a Utah high school varsity coach, written
around a Mike Wall live Q and A clip, walking through five seasons of his own
setter-by-setter data and the offensive-system decision each season produced.
It is the most concrete statistical treatment of the 5-1 versus 6-2 question in
this batch, and it is the source that turns Wall's rule of thumb ("only run a
6-2 if you have the players to justify a 6-2") into a documented method.

The method is: track attacking stats broken down by setter, separate first-ball
sideout from transition, and let the split decide. Across the seasons described,
the same method produced a full traditional 6-2 (2020), a straight 5-1 (2021 to
2022), a "modified 5-1" where one setter takes front-row rotations and the other
takes back-row rotations (2023), a hybrid where the primary setter also hit and a
second setter entered in the back row only sometimes (2024), and a return to a
straight 5-1 after a five-match 6-2 trial (2025).

The underlying stat tables are images in the original post and are not captured
in the raw snapshot; the narrative conclusions survive, the raw numbers do not.

## Key claims / ideas

- GMS (Mike Wall): "Don't do a 6-2 just to do a 6-2. Only do a 6-2 if you have
  the players to justify a 6-2."
- GMS: coaches should keep attacking stats broken down by each setter, and the
  5-1 versus 6-2 decision should be made from that data rather than from
  impression. When coaches have those stats, almost always one setter is clearly
  better than the other.
- GMS: separate first-ball sideout from transition when comparing setters. In
  the 2023 season described, the two setters were nearly identical in FBSO while
  one significantly outperformed the other in transition offense, and transition
  was the deciding column.
- GMS: watch for attempt asymmetry in the sample. The setter who starts each set
  accumulates far more setting attempts than the setter who starts on the bench,
  so raw attempt counts are not evidence of who is better.
- GMS: the 2020 team ran a traditional 6-2 in 115 of 116 sets, both setters and
  both opposites justified it statistically, and the team entered the state
  championship match with the longest winning streak in Utah. In hindsight the
  coach would run the same offense with the other setter starting.
- GMS: a "modified 5-1" is a working variant. One setter runs the front-row
  rotations, the other runs the back-row rotations, and they sub for each other.
  It stays a 5-1 in structure while two setters participate.
- GMS: a second variant lets the primary setter attack. The primary setter runs a
  5-1, and a second setter enters in the back row some of the time so the primary
  can show his attacking. In the 2024 boys season this produced higher hitting
  efficiency in transition when the second setter set (partly because he could set
  to the primary) and finished as a state title.
- GMS: implementation is not a binary switch. If the stats justify a 6-2, the
  coach still has to decide whether it is always, sometimes, only in certain
  rotations, only in certain phases, and which setter starts.
- GMS: 6-2 offenses work, especially at the high school level, and a 6-2 run
  without the players to justify it is damaging.

## Topics covered

- The 5-1 versus 6-2 decision as a data question rather than a philosophy
  question.
- Setter-by-setter attacking statistics and how to read them.
- FBSO versus transition offense as separate evaluation phases.
- Modified 5-1 and hybrid setter arrangements.
- Sample-size and starting-rotation bias in setter comparisons.
- High school varsity roster realities in system selection.
