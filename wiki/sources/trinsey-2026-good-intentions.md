# Good Intentions

## Summary

The expansion of the Winkleman thread from [[trinsey-2026-summer-school-2026]], and Trinsey's clearest statement of an intent-first coaching position. His claim is that intention is the single most important determinant of how the body moves, which makes influencing and verifying intent a higher-priority coaching job than delivering the cue itself.

The failure mode he names is specific and recognizable. Many athletes' operative intention drifts from "win the point" to "play the way I'm supposed to play," performing actions that resemble the game in the way it was taught to them. His diagnostic is the question "what were you trying to do there?", and the tell is an athlete who answers by talking about her feet.

The prescription runs distal to proximal. Start with the big, macro-level intention, and move the athlete's attention inward toward mechanics only when the macro intention fails to produce change. Trinsey explicitly rejects the reverse logic, which is that since some athletes will eventually need the detail, you may as well start there.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: intention is the single most important determinant of how the body moves. Coaches should prioritize influencing an athlete's intent and constantly check for understanding of it.
- Trinsey: many athletes' operative intention becomes "play the way I'm supposed to play" rather than "play to win the point," performing actions that resemble the game as it has been taught to them. He identifies this as a real performance problem rather than a framing quibble.
- Trinsey: the wall-push exercise demonstrates the mechanism. Pushing a wall to make as little noise as possible, to make a lot of noise, as if it were brittle and you want to chop it, as if it were heavy and you want to topple it, and as if you want to push yourself away all produce different acceleration, contact points, and stances with no technical instruction given.
- Trinsey: checking for understanding of intention is a separate diagnostic from checking understanding of instruction. An athlete can explain a concept back correctly and still not have changed her intention. His question is "what were you trying to do there?" and his warning sign is an answer about footwork.
- Trinsey: he prefers to start distal and get athletes focused on big intentions, moving more proximal only for athletes who do not respond. The trap is reasoning that since some athletes will eventually need minutia, you should start with minutia.
- Trinsey: his distal-to-proximal serving ladder runs from a spot on the floor where you want the ball, to a spot above the net where you want it to cross, to imagining walking down a tunnel as you approach.
- Trinsey: a "natural" at a sport is an athlete for whom distal macro-level intentions reliably improve performance and who does not get distracted by minutia. The same macro cue fails predictably for a small player on a big net (hitting hard just puts the ball out) or an athlete with a weak or injured shoulder.
- Trinsey: his passing intention ladder starts with "pass a ball your teammate can hand-set," because most athletes have surprisingly little awareness of what the setter actually did with the ball. The next, more proximal intention is passing into the middle third, on the observation that most athletes pass better linearly (back toward where the serve came from) than they redirect laterally. Once those intentions are in place, mechanical feedback has a better chance of landing in context.
- Trinsey: the line between a cue and an intention blurs, and the most effective cues are often intentions outright. "Smash through the ball" is both. He also notes that a radar gun creates a very distal intention and almost always changes how athletes attack the ball, sometimes for the better and sometimes not, because it is hard for an athlete to ignore.
