# Do Habits Exist? Part 2

## Summary

Part 2 of three ([[trinsey-2026-do-habits-exist-part-1]], [[trinsey-2026-do-habits-exist-part-3]]) and the most directly actionable of the series. Trinsey imports Duhigg's Keystone Habits concept and argues that the habits worth spending explicit instruction on are the ones that change what an athlete perceives, because perception then reshapes many downstream habits at no additional instructional cost.

His two worked examples are BSBH and setter release footwork. BSBH qualifies as a Keystone Habit because changing the eye sequence changes what the blocker sees and therefore how she moves; he notes that athletes trained on a traditional GMS BSBH sequence tend to show a "hold and read" posture at the net with a deliberate head turn to the setter, while athletes on a different sequence shift and move differently. The setter example is the December-to-February change from Part 1, worked backward: rather than installing better footwork at the point of contact, he intervened on pre-serve posture, overlap alignment, and release timing, and the footwork followed.

The piece also states his position on the direct-instruction question with unusual clarity: a pure motor-program view misses the perception piece, and a purely ecological view that shies away from explicit instruction misses that changing initial posture, setup, and timing feeds back into what the athlete perceives.

## Key claims / ideas

- Trinsey: Keystone Habits, habits that influence many other habits, are where explicit instruction pays off. Habits at the point of contact are very difficult to change, because what the athlete sees in front of her at that moment strongly determines how she moves.
- Trinsey: eye sequences are good candidates for Keystone Habits, and BSBH is one. Athletes trained on a traditional GMS BSBH sequence tend to adopt a hold-and-read stance at the net with a deliberate head turn to the setter; athletes trained on a different eye sequence shift and move differently.
- Trinsey: cues that occur before the whistle are far more reliable than cues inside the rally. Finding cues within the game is what expert performance is ultimately about, and pre-whistle cues are the easy win to take first, in the same spirit as the Organization-Mechanics-Skill ordering.
- Trinsey: setter release footwork gets less important as level rises and is basically irrelevant for professionals, who can release on the toss rather than on contact. It stays important for coaches of young players because their environment demands it.
- Trinsey: his explicit setter teaching sequence is to look at your overlap and place your right foot a half square back (readable off the sportcourt squares), keep the right foot well out in front of the left to encourage a big running first step, then look at the server, then time the release so movement starts before contact. You are not overlapped when the back foot leaves the ground; you are overlapped when the first step strikes the ground.
- Trinsey: the timing arithmetic that justifies the intervention is that a sprinter's block start takes about 0.3 seconds and a decent float serve takes about 1.0 seconds to reach the passers, so roughly a third of the serve flight is consumed before a setter who starts on contact has her first step down.
- Trinsey: when an action reliably produces a specific result, very little instruction is needed (babies learn light switches unprompted). When action and result are separated by probability and randomness, more specific instruction is needed. A poor setter release only clearly costs a few points a match and its sub-catastrophic effects get confused with other parts of setting, so the environment cannot supply a clean stimulus to change it.
- Trinsey: explicit instruction is powerful and hard-capped by how much the conscious brain can process at once, so the more of it that goes to Keystone Habits, the more effect it has.
