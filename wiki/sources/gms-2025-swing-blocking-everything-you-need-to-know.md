# Swing Blocking: Everything You Need To Know

## Summary

GMS's full teaching article on swing blocking, dated 2025-01-30. It is the
organization's most complete public statement of the technique: principles,
step-by-step footwork and armwork keys, the common traps GMS sees, and an
explicit position on when to start teaching it.

Two things make this source unusually useful for age-ladder work. First, GMS
states the cost of the technique out loud, that blocking is not the most
important skill in high school volleyball and that time spent on swing blocking
is time not spent on serving and receiving, and then argues the payoff anyway.
Second, it ties the value of blocking to the power of the opposition, which
gives a principled reason for allocating less blocking time at younger ages
rather than a purely developmental one.

The article is also a women's-game data point: GMS states that swing blocking
was historically a men's technique and that over the past 10 to 15 years nearly
all high-level women's teams have adopted it as their primary blocking move.

## Key claims / ideas

- GMS: has recommended swing blocking for all levels and genders for as long as
  the organization has existed, and an overwhelming majority of teams worldwide
  now use it. Historically a men's technique, adopted by women's players over the
  past 10 to 15 years; today nearly all high-level women's teams use it as their
  primary blocking move.
- GMS: the biomechanical case is greater jump height, greater blocking area,
  greater hand penetration, a shorter time from point A to point B than a
  traditional shuffle move, and more efficient adjusting along the net.
- GMS: the cost is stated explicitly. Blocking is not the most important skill in
  high school volleyball; swing blocking takes longer to learn than regular
  blocking, and every minute on it is a minute off serving and receiving. GMS
  keeps it in the curriculum because the long-run payoff is judged worth it.
- GMS: four blocking principles frame the technique. Swing blocking lets blockers
  move faster and jump higher; hitters hit where the set takes them; early vision
  leads to faster movements; simplicity equals repeatability.
- GMS: the primary footwork pattern is the 3-step crossover. Key 1a, small first
  step with the lead leg (many of the best swing blockers of both sexes take a
  negative step here). Key 1b, big second step with the crossover leg, which is
  where speed and power to cover ground come from. Key 1c, third step squares to
  the hitter, an intermediate position that prevents windmilling and avoids
  braking. Key 1d, square to the net at the reach.
- GMS: armwork keys are hands down at waist level on the first step, a double arm
  lift on the second step, a slight elbow bend when jumping to avoid the net and
  speed the hands over, and one fast press over the net. "Being over is more
  important than being high."
- GMS: on landing, GMS is neutral about proactively teaching a split landing. If
  the footwork keys are done properly the athlete experiences natural rotation and
  lands facing into the court on her own, though there are times when teaching it
  directly is warranted.
- GMS: five common traps are arms staying or waving high at head level on the
  first step, a big slow first step, squaring to the sideline instead of the
  hitter, a slow loopy swing over the net instead of a compact one with bent
  elbows, and the double move (reaching high, then over) that puts the hands in
  position after the ball has passed.
- GMS: on install age, there is a strong correlation between power and the value
  of blocking, so the less powerful the team the less blocking matters. Blocking
  for a U14 team is less impactful than at the Olympic level. GMS recommends
  introducing 3-step crossover fundamentals early without spending enormous
  amounts of time on them, and increasing the allocation as athletes get older and
  stronger.

## Topics covered

- Swing blocking versus traditional square-to-the-net shuffle blocking.
- Biomechanical rationale: jump height, blocking area, hand penetration, speed.
- The 3-step crossover footwork pattern, key by key.
- Armwork sequence and the press over the net.
- Middle blocker versus pin blocker differences in second-step size.
- Common teaching traps and their corrections.
- Age of introduction and the power-to-blocking-value relationship.
- The opportunity cost of blocking time against serving and passing time.
