# Add conditioning to tryouts to evaluate work ethic

## Summary

Jared Rudiger (head coach, Fort Collins High School) proposes conditioning as a work-ethic measurement instrument inside tryouts. Run a mile, sprints, push-ups, or burpees, and watch for maximum effort. His important qualifier is that effort and fitness are separate readings: the athletes putting in maximum effort are not necessarily the athletes in the best physical shape, so the coach scores the effort rather than the finishing order.

He adds a second-order use. After the conditioning block, reintroduce skill drills and observe how candidates execute technique while fatigued. That converts the conditioning block from a standalone fitness test into a fatigue manipulation applied to skill evaluation.

**This source is ingested as a contrary view.** It sits against the skill-and-behavior-based design of this wiki's tryout rubrics. See Contradictions below.

## Key claims / ideas

- Rudiger: incorporating conditioning into tryouts is a "foolproof" way to measure an athlete's work ethic.
- Rudiger: the specific modality is open, a mile, sprints, push-ups, burpees, or another workout all serve the purpose.
- Rudiger: maximum effort does not always correlate with who is in the best physical shape, so the coach scores observed effort rather than performance ranking.
- Rudiger: after conditioning, reincorporate skill drills to see how players adapt to skill work while fatigued.
- The underlying position is that work ethic is directly observable through a physical-exertion task, rather than being inferred from behavior during skill work.

## Contradictions

This wiki's tryout rubrics score demonstrated skill and observable in-gym behavior, and they explicitly warn against evaluating attributes the gym is not actually showing. Three specific tensions:

- **Work ethic is scored through coachability behaviors, not conditioning output.** [[tryout-rubric-14u]] locates work ethic and effort inside the "Mental and coachability" row, whose anchors are self-correction after one cue, effort held independent of score, in-rally communication, and engagement during teammate reps. Rudiger's instrument is a conditioning block instead. Both claim to measure the same construct through different evidence.
- **Fatigue-loaded skill evaluation cuts against a scoring protocol built on clean blocks.** The 14U rubric's calibration notes prescribe scoring after a block ends and warn that a candidate may be misreading the drill format rather than lacking the skill. Deliberately evaluating skill under fatigue raises the share of scores driven by conditioning state.
- **Conditioning-based selection is exposed to the growth-spurt and physical-projection biases the rubric names.** [[tryout-rubric-14u]] lists projection (scoring physical upside over demonstrated skill) and the growth-spurt confound as biases to avoid. A mile run or a burpee set loads exactly the athlete characteristics those cautions target, which is a sharper problem at 12s to 14s than at the high school level Rudiger is writing for.

Do not resolve the disagreement on the page. Present Rudiger's position with attribution alongside the rubric's, and note that his context is high school tryouts with a large, fitness-heterogeneous candidate pool.
