# Game-like serve training

## Summary

Diane Flick-Williams (Western Washington University) makes a specificity argument about how serving reps are distributed in practice. Blocked serving volume, a player standing at the line hitting ten balls in a row, does not match the distribution the game imposes. In a match a player serves one ball, then rotates, and may not serve again for ten minutes. Her fix is structural: give each player a single serve, then move to the next player, and wrap the single serve in a pressure situation.

The design does two things at once. It matches the game's rep spacing, which is a motor-learning argument about contextual interference, and it matches the game's psychological load, which is a pressure-inoculation argument. A player who has only ever served in comfortable blocked sets has practiced a skill the match never asks for.

Note the URL slug says "serving fundamentals" while the published title is "Game-like serve training"; the content is practice design rather than technique.

## Key claims / ideas

- Flick-Williams: consecutive serving repetitions in practice are useful but are not the most game-like distribution of reps.
- Flick-Williams: in a match a player may serve one ball and then not serve again for roughly ten minutes, so practice should reproduce that spacing.
- Her format gives each player exactly one serve before rotating to the next player.
- The single serve is deliberately placed inside a pressure situation, so the rep carries match-like consequence as well as match-like spacing.
