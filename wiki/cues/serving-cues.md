# Serving cues

## Overview

Serving is the most coachable skill in volleyball — every player serves, every miss is observable, every disruption is repeatable. The cue vocabulary the staff uses on the service line gets repeated more times per practice than for any other skill, which makes consistency across coaches load-bearing. This dictionary establishes the canonical phrasing.

The cues lean modern AOC across Mattox's Lift/Step/Swing rhythm cue, Dunning's toss-first emphasis, and Kiraly's pressure-rehearsal vocabulary [aoc-2025-float-serve-3-keys][aoc-2024-float-serve-toss][aoc-2018-dunning-serve-warmup][aoc-2024-kiraly-serving-pressure]. Munciana's working-club layer supplies the target accountability, the serve-selection vocabulary, and the practice architecture: aim small/miss small, hazard balls, stay out of the gut, five-before-ten, and the rule that a five-minute serving block sits on both sides of every water break [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Liskevych's ASICS serving instruction supplies the foundational technique vocabulary preserved across both modern and legacy traditions [aoc-2017-liskevych-asics-serving].

Jump float gets its own subsection below. It is the headline install from 13s through 15s in a float-dominant build, and the dictionary previously had no cues for it at all.

Cues are organized in the order a serve fails: toss goes wrong first (in the wrong place, too high, too low), step-and-rhythm goes wrong second (no weight transfer, contact off-rhythm), contact goes wrong third (off the wrong part of the hand, behind the body), tactical decision goes wrong fourth (wrong target, wrong type for the moment).

## Per-subskill canonical cues

### Toss

**"Toss, then step."** Introduced 11s. Refined 13s+. The Dunning toss-first cue rendered as a sequence rule [aoc-2024-float-serve-toss]. Teaches that the toss leaves the hand *before* the forward step begins — Dunning's framing is explicit: "wait until after the toss to step forward; stepping after the toss helps achieve a low, flat trajectory that is harder to pass" [aoc-2024-float-serve-toss]. Does NOT teach a stationary toss-and-stand — the step is part of the motion, just sequenced after the toss release.

**"Toss in front."** Introduced 11s. Lifelong. The toss-placement cue captured directly from Dunning [aoc-2024-float-serve-toss]: "Toss the ball in front of you — this is the single biggest float-serve key." Teaches that the toss lands one or two feet in front of the server's hitting shoulder, not over the head and not behind. Does NOT teach a high toss — the toss height is moderate; the placement-in-front matters more than height.

**"Tossing is the worst skill in volleyball."** Introduced 12s. Lifelong. The Dunning frame quoted verbatim [aoc-2024-float-serve-toss]. Teaches that the toss is the most-neglected and most-important element of the float serve, and that toss-only reps (without a swing) are a legitimate practice tool. Does NOT teach a fixed toss style — the cue installs the *attention* on the toss as a discrete skill.

**"Tossing reps without serving."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. The Dunning isolated-toss-practice cue [aoc-2024-float-serve-toss]. Teaches that the toss can be drilled separately from the swing — repeat the toss to a target spot in front of the server, hundreds of reps, without ever swinging through. Does NOT teach replacing serving practice — toss-only practice is a *supplement*, not a substitute.

**"You can't cheat with your feet."** Introduced 10s. Lifelong. Lingenfelter's rationale for the kneeling serve warm-up, captured at 00:24:24: "You just want to work on a good toss is the best way for people to learn toss because you can't cheat with your feet" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches why the kneel drill exists: a standing server can hide a bad toss by stepping under it, and taking the feet away makes the toss the only variable left. Right-handers kneel on the left knee, serve over from the 10-foot line, and back up half a step every time they get one over [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. The single-leg version does the same job with a visible tell: "if it's a bad toss, you'll see it because your player wobbles and they kind of tend to fall over" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Does NOT teach kneeling as a serving position: it is a constraint that isolates the toss, and it comes off as soon as the toss is honest.

**"Off the 10 by the end of the minute."** Introduced 10s. Refined 12s+. Munciana's serving-readiness diagnostic from the single-leg toss drill at 00:25:04: "I ask for the players to get as far back as they can. Some players won't get off the 10-foot line. That tells you that they're not where they need to be serving-wise, you need to work on it" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches the staff, more than the player: a one-minute drill where servers back up half a step per successful serve turns into a free roster-wide assessment. A player who cannot leave the 10-foot line is not a player with a toss problem, she is a player who needs dedicated serving development, and you now know it in the first week rather than in March. Does NOT teach the 10-foot line as a standard for anything else: it is a screen, not a target.

### Rhythm & motion

**"Lift, Step, Swing."** Introduced 12s. Refined 13s+. The Mattox three-word mantra captured verbatim from the AOC source: "Three-word mantra the server says aloud: Lift, Step, Swing" [aoc-2025-float-serve-3-keys]. Teaches the verbal-rhythm sequence the server says (often aloud) on each rep. Lift = the tossing arm raises with the ball. Step = right-handers step with the left foot, left-handers step with the right [aoc-2025-float-serve-3-keys]. Swing = contact the ball. Does NOT teach a metronomic equal-tempo execution — the swing accelerates through contact.

**"Contact right after the foot lands."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. The Mattox tempo cue captured directly: "the hitting hand should contact the ball immediately after the forward-stepping foot lands" [aoc-2025-float-serve-3-keys]. Teaches the weight-transfer rhythm — foot down, then contact, in a tight one-two. Does NOT teach a delayed contact — the gap between foot landing and contact is small.

**"Start slow, finish fast."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. A pacing cue applied to the serving motion. Teaches that the toss arm rises slowly, the swing accelerates through contact, and the entire motion is rhythm-not-rush. Echoes the Schmidt approach-tempo cue from hitting [aoc-2024-attack-approach]. Does NOT teach a slow swing — the finish is fast; the start is the part that's slow.

### Contact

**"Contact out front."** Introduced 11s. Lifelong. Teaches that the hitting hand makes contact in front of the body, not over the head. A contact behind the head produces a serve that drops short or sails long. Does NOT teach reaching forward across the shoulder line — contact stays in the shoulder's vertical plane, just slightly forward of the torso.

**"Heel of the hand, ball on the strings."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. The Liskevych hand-shape cue for the float serve [aoc-2017-liskevych-asics-serving]. Teaches that the contact happens with the heel of the hand and the ball makes a "thumping" not "popping" sound — the goal is a wobbling, no-spin float. Does NOT teach an open palm — the wrist is firm at contact, not loose.

**"Firm wrist."** Introduced 11s. Refined 13s+. The wrist-firmness cue for the float serve, captured in Nelson's youth-fundamentals corpus [aoc-2024-kids-serving-fundamentals]: "Wrist firmness for floaters vs. wrist extension for topspin is a core teaching distinction." Teaches that the float serve's wrist locks at contact — no snap, no follow-through arc. Does NOT teach a stiff arm — the firmness is wrist-only; the arm continues to swing through.

**"Snap through for topspin."** Introduced 16s. Refined 17s+. The wrist-action cue for the topspin serve [aoc-2024-kids-serving-fundamentals]. In this program the women's-game build runs float-dominant through 15s, with topspin entering at 16s for the most advanced servers. Teaches that the topspin serve uses wrist extension at contact to impart forward rotation — the opposite of the float-serve's firm wrist. Nelson is explicit: "the topspin mirrors the hitting arm swing" [aoc-2024-kids-serving-fundamentals]. Does NOT teach the same swing as the float — the two serves are mechanically distinct, and the cues are kept separate.

### Jump float

The dictionary carried no jump-float cues through earlier revisions, which was a real gap: jump float is the headline serving install from 13s through 15s in this program, and the float-dominant build means it is the serve most of the roster will actually own. See [[serving-jump-float]] for the technique page. The cues below extend the standing-float vocabulary rather than replacing it, which is the point: a server who has "Toss in front" and "Lift, Step, Swing" already has most of the jump float.

**"Same toss, moving."** Introduced 13s. Refined 15s+. The gate cue for the jump float: a server does not add the approach until she can put the toss in the same place while her body is travelling forward. Dunning's standing-float principle is the standard the moving toss has to meet: the toss goes out in front, and better tosses are what let players "control the contact of the ball" [aoc-2024-float-serve-toss]. On a jump float the toss also has to be released early enough that the server catches up to it rather than reaching back. Use Dunning's isolated-toss reps here with the approach added and no swing at all: toss, step, land, catch [aoc-2024-float-serve-toss]. **The gating rule itself (no jump float until the moving toss is repeatable) is program convention [unsourced].** Does NOT teach a higher toss for the jump float: the placement discipline is the same; only the release timing moves.

**"Lift, step, swing, in the air."** Introduced 13s. Refined 15s+. Mattox's three-word mantra stretched across the approach [aoc-2025-float-serve-3-keys]. Lift is the toss, released as the first step goes down. Step becomes the approach step and the takeoff rather than a single weight transfer. Swing is a contact taken in the air, in front, with the same firm wrist as the standing float. Mattox's tempo rule survives the translation and is the thing to coach: the hitting hand contacts the ball immediately after the stepping foot lands, which on a jump float means the takeoff and the contact are one continuous beat rather than a jump followed by a swing [aoc-2025-float-serve-3-keys]. Does NOT teach a hitting approach: the jump float is a short, controlled, mostly-vertical lift, and a server who runs a full attack approach at the end line arrives late and behind the ball.

**"Land where you took off."** Introduced 14s. Refined 16s+. Teaches that a jump float that drifts forward has converted the server's control into momentum she cannot use: contact drifts, the wrist loosens to compensate, and the serve either sails or dives. The correction is the same shape as the hitting cue "Jump up, not out," and pairing the two phrases across skills is deliberate. Does NOT teach a standing jump: there is real forward travel in the step; the takeoff just converts it upward instead of carrying it over the line.

### Target accountability

**"Aim small, miss small."** Introduced 11s. Lifelong. The Munciana serving philosophy quoted verbatim from Lingenfelter's camp library: "One of the biggest things with serving is aim small, miss small. We got too much area to look at. So I want to find something that's real finite. And so aim small, miss small" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches that every serve targets a finite location (Lingenfelter's library uses physical objects: noodles in a specific seam, cones, painted spots — down to a 2-inch area at the high end). Does NOT teach a corner-only target rule — the small target moves with tactical context.

**"Aim noodle high."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. The specific Munciana drill cue from Lingenfelter's noodle-target progression [munciana-2022-camp-drills]: "We put the noodle in a certain area. You can put it wherever you want. We put it about 18 inches above the net. And we aim for the noodle. So we want our players to really tune in and aim noodle high." Teaches a specific net-height target on a specific seam (the 5-6 seam, or whichever the drill names). Does NOT teach a fixed serve trajectory — the noodle moves; the cue installs the precision habit.

**"Top of tape, second red."** Introduced 13s. Refined 15s+. The Munciana low-tough-serve target cue captured directly from Lingenfelter [munciana-2022-camp-drills]: "I want that tough serve aiming between the top of the tape and the second red. ... Every time they serve, they need to be looking and find the top of the tape and the second red just so they have a barometer or an idea of where they want to go." Teaches that the toughness of a serve comes from net-clearance height: between the top of the net tape and the second red stripe on the antenna — flat trajectory that lands in deep zones with minimal arc. Does NOT teach a low-arc rule for every serve — the cue is for the *tough serve* type; high-arc safe serves are a different category.

**"Hit the seam."** Introduced 13s. Refined 15s+. The serve-target cue for tactical placement: serve to the seam between two passers (typically zones 5-6 between left-back and middle-back, or 1-2 between right-back and middle-back) so the passers have to communicate and one will hesitate. Does NOT teach a specific seam — the seam moves with the serve-receive formation; the cue is to *find* the seam in the moment.

**"Make it or miss it."** Introduced 11s. Lifelong. Lingenfelter's basketball framing, captured at 00:27:20 during the seam-strike drill: "It's like basketball. If all goes in, it's good. If it doesn't, it's not. We need to create the same mindset on serving. So we don't want to serve randomly. We want to serve with purpose in everything we do" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches that a serve has a binary outcome against its *target*, not against the court: a serve that lands in but nowhere near the intended spot is a miss, and calling it one is what makes serving practice honest. The analogy does the work that a lecture on accountability cannot, because every player already knows a shot either goes in or it doesn't. Does NOT teach binary judgment of the player: the shot is graded, not the server.

### Serve selection

**"Hazard balls."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. Munciana's name for the two serves worth owning, captured at 00:29:03: "The ball served deep, and the ball served extremely short are two hazard balls. Those are balls we want to be able to serve effectively in the game. The best way to practice what you want in the game is to practice it in practice" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches that the two ends of the court are where a serve actually hurts a passing platform, and that both are trainable as discrete targets rather than as accidents. Lingenfelter drills the deep one directly with the bounce game (serve a ball that one-hops into a box sitting on the end line) and the short one through target placement [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Does NOT teach avoiding the rest of the court: hazard balls are the two serves you build on purpose; seam serves and zone serves remain the everyday tools.

**"Stay out of the gut."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. The No-Gut Serving rule, captured at 00:29:37: "We don't want to serve the middle of the court because that's where the best passer usually sets" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches the negative constraint that produces court-width serving: the middle is off limits, so servers have to learn to move the ball wide on a regular basis. The scoring makes the priority explicit and is worth copying verbatim: "One for the gut, five for an air wide or long, ten for an air in the net" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Note the ordering, because it is the whole philosophy in three numbers: a soft serve into the middle is a penalty, a miss long or wide is a bigger one, and a serve into the net is the worst outcome on the court because it removes any chance of scoring. Does NOT teach an absolute prohibition: the gut is a one-push-up mistake, which is the point; it is discouraged, not banned.

**"Don't take the safety serve."** Introduced 13s. Refined 15s+. Lingenfelter's name for what the gut rule is really correcting, at 00:30:48: "It allows them to develop a serve that gets away from the safety serve. The ball served in the middle of the court is a safety serve. It's them trying to protect themselves" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches the staff to read a middle-of-the-court serve as a self-protection choice rather than a technical one, which changes the correction: the fix is a target and a consequence structure, not a mechanical adjustment. His framing of the goal is "smart servers as opposed to going back and just being a little bit afraid of what's going on" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Does NOT teach punishing fear: the drill design absorbs the fear by making the safe choice cost something small and predictable.

### Aggression & pressure

**"Five before ten."** Introduced 13s. Refined 15s+. The Munciana aggressive-mentality drill cue captured directly from Lingenfelter [munciana-2022-camp-drills]: "We're going to go five before ten. Five let serves before you accumulate ten errors. So you're looking to get five lets, top of the net and over. ... I want that aggressive serving mentality and you need to practice this and you're going to harp on errors." Teaches the aggressive serving mentality: a server's job is to threaten the pass, not to land the ball safely. The drill rewards lets (net touches that go over) before errors accumulate. Lingenfelter's commentary frames the philosophy: "the other [way to create speed] is to serve the ball really low" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Does NOT teach reckless serving — the cue is about *intent* under a structured drill, not free-form risk.

**"Just me and the ball."** Introduced 14s. Refined 16s+. The Reid Priddy mantra captured in Kiraly's pressure-serving article [aoc-2024-kiraly-serving-pressure]: "Example cue — Reid Priddy: 'Just me and the ball.'" Teaches a personal pre-serve mantra that narrows attention and blocks out crowd noise, scoreboard pressure, and self-talk. Kiraly's framing: "The aim is to block out noise and perform the skill 'without fear of failure'" [aoc-2024-kiraly-serving-pressure]. Does NOT teach this specific phrase as the only mantra — the cue installs the *practice* of having a personal mantra; the words are individual.

**"Serve to score, not to start."** Introduced 10s. Refined 14s+. The Nelson youth-fundamentals frame applied as a cue [aoc-2024-kids-serving-fundamentals]: "Teach serving with intent to score, not just 'over the net.'" Teaches that the serve is the first attack, not an opening ritual. Does NOT teach a kill-or-error rule — the cue installs the mental frame, not a specific risk profile.

**"Heckle while she serves."** Introduced 15s. Refined 17s+. The Kiraly pressure-rehearsal coaching cue rendered as a practice instruction [aoc-2024-kiraly-serving-pressure]: "Simulate match pressure in practice by having teammates heckle the server during reps." Teaches that pressure is rehearsable — the team imposes match-pressure conditions during serving reps so the server's mechanics, ritual, and mantra all hold under noise. Does NOT teach hostile coaching — the heckle is *rehearsal*, not punishment.

### Pre-serve routine

**"Same routine every time."** Introduced 13s. Lifelong. Teaches that every serve starts with the same pre-serve ritual — bounce count, breath, cue word — so the server's body and attention are in the same state on every rep. The ritual is a stability anchor under pressure. Does NOT teach a fixed ritual content — the bounce count, the breath, the cue word are individual; the consistency is universal. *Coverage gap: the 13s age-guide does not yet carry pre-serve-routine content to match this introduction age, so a 13s coach reading the guide alone would not know to install it.*

**"Read the receiver before the toss."** Introduced 14s. Refined 16s+. Teaches that the server takes a beat to scan the receiving formation — passer positions, the libero's location, who is hiding — before committing to a toss. Does NOT teach a long deliberation — the read is a glance, not a study.

### Warm-up

**"Start at the 10, walk back."** Introduced 10s. Refined 13s+. The Dunning serving-warmup protocol captured directly [aoc-2018-dunning-serve-warmup]: "Start at the 10-foot line, progressively move back to the service line. ... Start serving slow and short before moving back and adding speed." Teaches the staged warm-up that builds shoulder readiness before full-distance serves begin. Does NOT teach skipping the warm-up — the cue installs the *practice* of staged warm-up before live serving.

**"Open practice with serving."** Introduced 14s. Refined 16s+. The Mattox practice-design cue [aoc-2022-mattox-serving-warmup]: "If you put serving first, you tell the team serving matters. ... Warmups reveal priorities: what you put first is what you say matters." Teaches that the practice block ordering signals priority — serving first reinforces serving's importance in the team's mental model. Does NOT teach a fixed practice template — the cue is a periodization principle, applied where the season-context calls for it.

**"Serving brackets every water break."** Introduced 10s. Lifelong. Munciana's structural rule for serving volume, captured at 00:31:05: "Usually last, all of our serving drills will last five minutes. They almost always end the focus section. ... Before we get water, we always serve. After we get water, we always serve. So serving is a vital part of the game" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches the scheduling mechanic that makes Mattox's priority-signaling point survive a real practice plan: five-minute serving blocks are attached to the water breaks, so serving volume accumulates without ever needing to be defended against a coach who wants the court for something else. Every break is a serving block on both sides of it. Does NOT teach five minutes as a fixed dose: the bracket is the rule, and the block length flexes with practice length.

## Anti-cues

- **"Just get it in."** Removes intent and target from the serve; produces servers who land easy serves the opponent passes 3.0. Modern correction: "Aim small, miss small" plus "Serve to score, not to start."
- **"Don't miss."** Negative cue that focuses attention on the failure outcome and produces tentative, under-aggressive servers. Modern correction: "Five before ten" — frames the aggressive serve as the goal and the error budget as part of the rep.
- **"Toss high."** Vague toss instruction; produces tosses that drift behind the server or float too long. Modern correction: "Toss in front" — placement matters more than height.
- **"Step into your serve."** Encourages stepping *during* or *before* the toss, contradicting the Dunning toss-then-step sequence. Modern correction: "Toss, then step."
- **"Snap your wrist on the float."** Wrong wrist mechanic for the float serve; produces topspin instead of float. Modern correction: "Firm wrist" for the float; "Snap through for topspin" only on topspin serves.
- **"Hit the corner."** Vague tactical instruction. Modern correction: "Hit the seam" or a specific zone target — the corner is a category, not an aim point.
- **"Serve hard."** Power-cue without a target; produces uncontrolled hard serves into the net or out of bounds. Modern correction: "Aim small, miss small" with "Top of tape, second red" for the toughness target.
- **"Don't think about it."** Encourages the server to bypass the routine that holds mechanics under pressure. Modern correction: "Same routine every time" plus "Just me and the ball."
- **"You can't miss this serve."** Pressure cue that backfires. Modern correction: "Five before ten" frames errors as part of an aggressive standard.

## Voice consistency notes

The serving cue vocabulary is one of the more converged areas across modern AOC and Munciana, with relatively little contested ground.

**Modern AOC** (Mattox's Lift/Step/Swing, Dunning's toss-first, Kiraly's pressure-rehearsal, Nelson's youth fundamentals) leads the program's working voice [aoc-2025-float-serve-3-keys][aoc-2024-float-serve-toss][aoc-2024-kiraly-serving-pressure][aoc-2024-kids-serving-fundamentals]. The cue language is heavily quoted verbatim from the AOC corpus.

**Munciana** is the strongest distinctive voice in this skill, and the camp tape's serving section is its densest ten minutes on any topic. Aim small/miss small, the noodle-target progression, the top-of-tape-second-red toughness target, five-before-ten, hazard balls, stay out of the gut, the safety-serve diagnosis, the you-can't-cheat-with-your-feet kneel drill, the make-it-or-miss-it basketball framing, and the rule that serving brackets every water break are all Lingenfelter cues with no direct AOC equivalent at the verbatim level [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. The division of labor with AOC is clean: AOC owns the mechanics (toss, rhythm, contact), Munciana owns the targeting, the accountability structure, and the practice architecture. They do not overlap enough to conflict.

**GMS** does not contribute distinct cue vocabulary to serving so much as practice-design rationale: serving reps benefit from random/variable practice (varied targets, varied serve types, varied pressure conditions) rather than blocked rep volume. The cue language in this dictionary is compatible with GMS practice architecture; "Aim small, miss small" and "Five before ten" both work in random/variable formats.

**USAV / federation** vocabulary aligns with the modern AOC corpus on serving — Kiraly's pressure-rehearsal cue and the toss-first emphasis both have direct USAV-curriculum support.

**Russian / Brazilian / Italian men's traditions** prefer jump-topspin as the primary weapon and use a different cue cluster (jump-toss height, contact-at-apex, full-arm-action). The program does not adopt this cue vocabulary at the women's club / HS / college level because jump-topspin is not the primary tool; the cues are preserved on the [[serving-topspin]] technique page for context.

**Japanese tradition** trains float serving with extreme precision and a narrower toss window. The cue language overlaps with the modern AOC/Munciana frame; the distinctive Japanese contribution is the *volume* of toss-only reps, which the program adopts as a practice architecture without distinct cue vocabulary.

When schools genuinely disagree on a serving cue — for example, "snap the wrist on every serve for ball speed" (Russian power-tradition cue) versus "firm wrist for float, snap only for topspin" (modern AOC cue) — the dictionary keeps both with attribution. The program's working voice is the modern AOC/Munciana frame.

The phrases every coach in the program should say: toss in front, toss then step, you can't cheat with your feet, lift step swing, contact right after the foot lands, contact out front, firm wrist, same toss moving (jump float), land where you took off, aim small miss small, top of tape second red, make it or miss it, hit the seam, hazard balls, stay out of the gut, five before ten, just me and the ball, serve to score not to start, same routine every time. Nineteen phrases. Repeat them.
