# Blocking cues

## Overview

Blocking is one of the higher-leverage skills to teach with cues because the moment-to-moment decisions are dense (read the setter, read the hitter, time the jump, position the hands, finish the press) and the time window is short. The cue vocabulary has converged in the last decade on a modern frame: **read drives everything**, **swing-block footwork is the default at HS and above**, and **press hands over the net** [aoc-2024-kiraly-reads-every-skill][aoc-2026-swing-blocking-footwork-beginners][aoc-2024-slabe-blocking].

The cues lean modern AOC across Slabe's hand-press teaching, Rosen's swing-block-for-beginners progression, Mardini's swing-blocking case, Sagula's static-blocking fundamentals, and Slabe's bunch-read system [aoc-2024-slabe-blocking][aoc-2026-swing-blocking-footwork-beginners][aoc-2024-importance-teaching-swing-blocking][aoc-2024-blocking-fundamentals][aoc-2026-bunch-read-blocking]. McCutcheon's blocking-keys corpus contributes middle-blocker independence and read-drives-system framing [aoc-2015-mccutcheon-blocking-keys]. Liskevych and Murczek supply the in-house dissent on whether swing-blocking earns its learning cost [aoc-2016-liskevych-blocking-what-matters][aoc-2023-swing-blocking-when].

**Sourcing note.** This dictionary carried an "outside foot high" pin-blocking entry attributed to Munciana's 2022 camp library through earlier revisions. That attribution was wrong. The quote is real, but Lingenfelter says it inside the Three-Way Release *perimeter-defense* drill, coaching back-court defenders on their sideline stagger, and the 2022 camp tape contains no blocking instruction at all. Those cues now live in [[defense-cues]] where they belong. The program's outside-foot-forward pin-blocking stance survives here as a program convention, tagged and queued for a real source.

Cues are organized in the order a block fails: read goes wrong first (eyes on the wrong cue, late commit); footwork goes wrong second (late close, off-balance plant); hands go wrong third (low press, cross-the-net early, no penetration). Order the cue language to that failure order.

## Per-subskill canonical cues

### Read

**"Ball-setter-ball-hitter."** Introduced 13s. Refined 15s+. The canonical Kiraly eye-progression cue [aoc-2024-kiraly-reads-every-skill][kiraly-1997-championship-volleyball]. Teaches the four-step eye sequence: see the ball off the pass, find the setter, return to the ball as it leaves the setter's hands, then commit eyes to the hitter at the apex. Compresses the entire reading discipline into a single phrase. Does NOT teach staring at the hitter — eyes spend most of the read on the setter and the ball.

**"Read, then jump."** Introduced 13s. Lifelong. Teaches that the block's timing is set by the hitter's apex, not by the ball off the setter's hands. A blocker who jumps with the set is too early. The cue installs the patience to wait for the hitter's commitment before lifting. Does NOT teach a reactive jump — at higher levels the blocker is already committed by the time the hitter swings; the cue is about *not* jumping with the set.

**"What is the setter giving me?"** Introduced 14s. Refined 16s+. The Slabe / McCutcheon read-the-setter prompt rendered as a cue [aoc-2015-mccutcheon-blocking-keys][aoc-2024-slabe-blocking]. Teaches that the setter's body position, hand-shape commit point, and eye-line all telegraph distribution before the ball releases. McCutcheon emphasizes middle-blocker independence: middles should read and block independently rather than always closing to the wing blocker [aoc-2015-mccutcheon-blocking-keys]. Does NOT teach a fixed read protocol — the cue forces the read; the answer varies.

### Footwork (static / shuffle)

**"Outside foot forward on the pin."** Introduced 14s. Refined 16s+. **Program convention [unsourced].** Teaches the pin blocker's closing stance: the outside foot (the one nearer the antenna) sits forward of the inside foot so the torso angles back into the court, which seals the line-side seam and lets the blocker rotate into the attack instead of reaching across her own body. Does NOT teach a square-stance pin block, and does NOT teach a permanently staggered ready position: the stagger belongs to the close, not to the base.

*Why this entry is tagged.* Earlier revisions of this dictionary attributed the phrase "outside foot high" to Lingenfelter's Munciana camp library as a blocking cue. Lingenfelter does say it, but he says it to back-court defenders inside the Three-Way Release perimeter drill, and the tape contains no blocking instruction. The pin-stance convention the program actually teaches is not sourced to anything in the wiki yet. Coaches should keep saying it, and the queue should keep hunting for a real citation. See [[defense-cues]] for the correctly-categorized defensive cue.

**"Step-close."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. The shuffle-block footwork cue. Teaches the basic two-step lateral pattern: outside foot steps, inside foot closes. The literacy footwork before swing-block is introduced. Does NOT teach a single-step block — at any meaningful pin distance, the blocker steps and closes.

**"Plant balanced."** Introduced 12s. Lifelong. Teaches that the block's plant arrives with weight evenly distributed and feet flat, not on the toes or off-balance. A blocker who plants on the toes lifts forward into the net; a blocker who plants off-balance produces a hand position that drifts at apex. Does NOT teach a passive plant — the plant is the loading position for the jump.

### Footwork (swing block)

**"Long, aggressive steps."** Introduced 12s (top-club, late season). Refined 13s-14s. The Rosen swing-block-for-beginners cue [aoc-2026-swing-blocking-footwork-beginners]. Teaches that the swing-block footwork uses long aggressive strides — sprint-arm pattern, full hip rotation, and lateral coverage that exceeds the static-shuffle pattern. Rosen's whole point is that the pattern is beginner-teachable, which is why the program introduces it to its strongest 12s in the back half of the season rather than holding it to HS. Does NOT teach a slow shuffle — swing block is the *acceleration* layer on top of the static literacy.

**"Footwork first, jump later."** Introduced 12s (top-club, late season). Refined 13s-14s. The Rosen progression cue captured directly: "Teach the footwork first without a jump; only add the jump once steps are comfortable" [aoc-2026-swing-blocking-footwork-beginners]. Teaches the staged installation of swing-block: first practice the steps from the 10-foot line without jumping, then add the jump once the footwork is reliable. Because the first stage has no jump in it, it is safe to install early, and that staging is exactly what makes a 12s introduction workable. Does NOT teach the entire swing-block pattern at once — the staged order is the teaching design.

**"Sprint-arm the close."** Introduced 14s. Refined 16s+. Teaches the swing-block arm action: arms swing aggressively forward and back like a sprinter through the lateral close, building momentum into the plant. Mardini's basketball-rim analogy makes the same point: "trying to touch a basketball rim, nobody stands still — they run and jump; swing blocking applies the same principle at the net" [aoc-2024-importance-teaching-swing-blocking]. Does NOT teach static arms — explicitly the opposite.

### Hands & jump

**"Hands at face level, ready before contact."** Introduced 11s. Lifelong. The Sagula static-blocking-fundamentals cue [aoc-2024-blocking-fundamentals]. Teaches that the blocker starts with hands held at face level (eye level), fingers spread, never below waist. A blocker whose hands start at waist level loses the time-to-press. Does NOT teach high static hands at all moments — the start-position rule is for the ready position, not for the apex.

**"Press over."** Introduced 12s. Lifelong. The Slabe penetrate-the-net hand-press cue [aoc-2024-slabe-blocking]. Teaches that at apex the hands extend over the net into the opposing court — fingers spread, thumbs up, palms angled down. The block is not a vertical wall; it is a press into the opponent's space. Does NOT teach reaching across — the press is straight forward over the net, not diagonally across to seal a seam (which is a separate cue).

**"Penetrate, don't cover."** Introduced 14s. Refined 16s+. The modern correction to a long-standing error pattern. Teaches that the blocker's hands extend *over* the net (into the opposing court) rather than reaching *back* to cover an attacker behind the net. A "covering" block produces a flat block face that absorbs the ball without redirecting it. Does NOT teach a static block — the hands still match the attacker's likely angle, but they do so while penetrating, not while retreating.

**"Thumbs up, palms down."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. The hand-shape cue at apex. Teaches the modern fingers-spread-stiff, thumbs-up, palms-angled-down configuration that maximizes block surface area and directs the ball downward. Does NOT teach flat hands or vertical palms — both produce a block that deflects rather than presses.

**"Land balanced, ready to transition."** Introduced 14s. Lifelong. Sagula's landing cue captured directly: "two keys to first-line defense: (1) jump and reach over the net (penetration); (2) land balanced and ready for transition" [aoc-2024-blocking-fundamentals]. Teaches that the block ends with a balanced landing that lets the blocker immediately enter transition footwork. Does NOT teach a stuck landing — the block-to-approach recovery starts at touchdown (see [[transition-cues]]).

### System cues

**"Bunch in, release on the read."** Introduced 14s as a concept. Applied 15s. Default 16s+. The Slabe bunch-read system cue [aoc-2026-bunch-read-blocking]. Teaches that the pin blockers start pinched in toward the middle to help on seam sets, then release to cover the pin once the set commits. Slabe is explicit that this scheme demands "dynamic, fast pin blockers who can read sets and attackers" — it is best suited to advanced levels [aoc-2026-bunch-read-blocking]. The program introduces the *idea* at 14s (why the pins start inside), runs it situationally at 15s, and makes it the default alignment at 16s and above. Does NOT teach a permanent pin position — the bunch is the *start*, not the destination.

**"Commit with the quick, not with the setter."** Introduced 14s. Refined 16s+. The commit-block timing cue. Teaches that a committing blocker jumps off the quick hitter's *load*, not off the setter's release: the entire point of commit is to be in the air with the attacker, and a middle who leaves the floor when the ball leaves the setter's hands is late on the quick and absent from everything else. McCutcheon's frame is that commit, read, and combination coverage are matchup choices made against a specific opponent rather than a season-long identity [aoc-2015-mccutcheon-blocking-keys]. Sagula's ready-position standard supplies the posture the commit jumps from: zone 3, hands at eye level, roughly six inches off the net [aoc-2024-blocking-fundamentals]. Does NOT teach committing every rally: commit needs a scouting basis, and [[commit-blocking]] covers when that basis exists.

**"Middle reads, doesn't follow."** Introduced 16s. Refined 17s+. The McCutcheon middle-blocker-independence cue [aoc-2015-mccutcheon-blocking-keys]. Teaches that the middle blocker's commit decision is hers — she reads the setter and goes where the read sends her, rather than always closing to the pin blocker. Does NOT teach a free-lance middle — the middle's reads exist within the team blocking scheme; independence is *within* the system, not outside it.

**"Stuff for points, not for tip."** Introduced 15s. Refined 17s+. The Slabe goal-of-blocking cue rendered tactically: "Blocking goal: stuff for points; not funnel, soft-block, or deflect" [aoc-2024-slabe-blocking]. Teaches that the block's primary objective is the terminal stuff, with deflection and tip-coverage as secondary outcomes. Does NOT teach abandoning soft-block defense — the cue is about *intent*, not about banning softer blocks situationally.

## Anti-cues

- **"Hands at the net."** Vague start-position cue; produces blockers with hands at waist or chest level. Modern correction: "Hands at face level, ready before contact" [aoc-2024-blocking-fundamentals].
- **"Jump with the set."** Times the block too early. Modern correction: "Read, then jump" — wait for the hitter's commitment.
- **"Reach over."** Used loosely, this turns into "reach across the net" rather than penetration. Modern correction: "Press over" — the press is forward, not diagonal.
- **"Square block."** Fine for some pin attacks but produces a non-sealing pin block on outside attacks. Modern correction: "Outside foot forward on the pin"; the square stance is a middle-block default, not a pin default.
- **"Go when the ball leaves the setter's hands."** On a commit block this is the timing error that wastes the commitment: the middle is already descending when the quick swings. Modern correction: "Commit with the quick, not with the setter," because the jump keys off the attacker's load.
- **"Block everything."** Blocking is the skill least correlated with winning, per McCutcheon's data: "Blocking is the skill least correlated with winning; prioritize serving, passing, and sideout in practice" [aoc-2015-mccutcheon-blocking-keys]. Modern correction: prioritize block touches as defense, not stuff-rate as the metric — the block sets up the dig, the dig sets up the transition.
- **"Soft-block when you can't get the stuff."** Encourages a soft-block default; Slabe's frame is the opposite [aoc-2024-slabe-blocking]. Modern correction: "Stuff for points, not for tip" — soft-block is situational, not the default.
- **"Always close to the pin."** The legacy middle-blocker rule. McCutcheon's modern frame says middles should be free to read independently [aoc-2015-mccutcheon-blocking-keys]. Modern correction: "Middle reads, doesn't follow."
- **"Time the block."** Vague timing instruction. Modern correction: "Read, then jump" — the timing is read-driven, not internal-clock-driven.
- **"Don't get tooled."** Negative cue that focuses attention on the failure outcome. Modern correction: "Press over" plus "Outside foot forward on the pin" — process cues that solve the underlying mechanic.

## Voice consistency notes

The blocking cue vocabulary is genuinely contested across schools, particularly on the swing-block-versus-static-block question.

**Modern AOC** (Slabe, Rosen, Mardini, Kiraly) leads the program's working voice on swing-block as the default footwork [aoc-2024-importance-teaching-swing-blocking][aoc-2026-swing-blocking-footwork-beginners][aoc-2024-slabe-blocking]. The cue language reflects this: "long, aggressive steps," "sprint-arm the close," "footwork first, jump later." The install ladder runs earlier than this dictionary once claimed. Static-block literacy goes in at 12s, the jumpless swing-block footwork drill follows in the back half of the 12s season for top-club teams, and 13s refines it. By 14s the full swing block is emerging for middles on top-club teams, so a 14s coach should already be using the swing-block vocabulary with those players rather than holding it back. At 15s it is the primary vocabulary for every blocker on the roster.

**AOC dissent within the modern frame.** Liskevych and Murczek both question whether swing-blocking produces the net penetration that actually matters. Liskevych calls it the "flavor of the month" and argues traditional technique is more durable, ranking hands, then balanced loading, then penetration as the priority order [aoc-2016-liskevych-blocking-what-matters]. Murczek's version is an opportunity-cost argument: the jump is higher but the reach over the net is not, and the months spent mastering the pattern may be better spent on attacking and passing [aoc-2023-swing-blocking-when]. The program's resolution: teach static-block literacy first, layer swing-block on top once footwork is stable, and preserve static-block cues ("step-close," "plant balanced") for blockers who never make the swing-block transition.

**Munciana supplies no blocking cue vocabulary to this dictionary.** The 2022 camp library contains no blocking instruction, and the "outside foot high" phrase this page once attributed to Lingenfelter as a pin-blocking cue is a perimeter-defense cue from the Three-Way Release drill (see [[defense-cues]]). The program's own outside-foot-forward pin stance stands as an unsourced program convention until a real citation is found.

**GMS** contributes the practice-architecture argument: blocking reads transfer better when trained inside random/variable practice rather than blocked progression from predetermined sets to full variability. The cue language is compatible — the dictionary's read cues ("Ball-setter-ball-hitter," "What is the setter giving me?") are external-focus prompts that work in any practice architecture.

**Japanese tradition** trains blocking as one layer of a defense-first system, with anticipation, timing, and precise feet compensating for shorter reach. The cue vocabulary at the technique level is similar; the distinctive Japanese contribution is at the *system* level — the block is expected to deflect more than stuff, with the floor defense reading off the block's shape. This matters less for cue language than for tactical framing.

**Ecological dynamics** prefers cues that emerge from constraint-led practice. "Read, then jump" and "Press over" are relational cues that work in any task; the dictionary's vocabulary is largely compatible with the eco-dynamics frame.

When schools genuinely disagree on a blocking cue — for example, "shuffle and lift" (legacy/Liskevych preference) versus "long aggressive steps with sprint arms" (modern AOC swing-block preference) — the dictionary keeps both with attribution and notes that the program's working voice from 14s upward is the modern swing-block frame [aoc-2016-liskevych-blocking-what-matters][aoc-2026-swing-blocking-footwork-beginners].

The phrases every coach in the program should say: ball-setter-ball-hitter, read then jump, what is the setter giving me, outside foot forward on the pin, step-close, hands at face level, press over, penetrate don't cover, thumbs up palms down, land balanced ready to transition, long aggressive steps (12s-14s install, 15s+ default), footwork first jump later (same ladder), commit with the quick not with the setter, middle reads doesn't follow, stuff for points not for tip. Fifteen phrases. Repeat them.
