# Defense cues

## Overview

Defense is a back-row reading-and-pursuit skill, and the cue vocabulary the staff uses on defensive reps determines whether players develop into elite diggers or stay average. The modern frame leads with **the Three P's of defense (posture, position, passion — Munciana)**, **Stone's hips-under-the-ball platform-management cue**, **Josephson's Look/See/Decide reading frame**, and **Kiraly's read-the-hitter-not-the-ball priority** [munciana-2022-camp-drills][aoc-2025-controlling-the-dig][aoc-2025-training-reading-look-see-decide][aoc-2024-kiraly-reads-every-skill].

The cues lean modern AOC across Stone's controlling-the-dig corpus, Josephson's look-see-decide compartmentalization, Liskevych's ASICS defense instruction, Flynn-Oldenburg's emergency-move inventory, and Kiraly's reading-as-part-of-every-skill frame [aoc-2025-controlling-the-dig][aoc-2025-training-reading-look-see-decide][aoc-2017-liskevych-asics-defense][aoc-2022-emergency-moves-passing-defense][aoc-2024-kiraly-reads-every-skill]. Munciana's working-club layer is the heaviest single contributor to this dictionary: the Three P's, the perimeter-base stagger, the read-and-run identity, and the checkable coverage standard all come from Lingenfelter's 2022 camp library [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Japanese-tradition cues are preserved where the dig-up-everything ethic carries vocabulary worth keeping.

The perimeter-base cues below ("outside foot high," "cut the outside foot," "seal the perimeter") were mis-filed in [[blocking-cues]] through earlier revisions of this wiki. They are defensive cues. Lingenfelter says them inside Three-Way Release, a back-court movement drill run with the DSs and outside hitters, and the camp tape contains no blocking instruction at all [munciana-2022-camp-drills].

Cues are organized in the order a defensive rep fails: read goes wrong first (eyes on the wrong cue, no commitment), pursuit goes wrong second (late-step, reach with the arms instead of the legs), platform goes wrong third (early formation, off-angle, hips not under the ball), and recovery-after-touch goes wrong fourth (ball goes up, no one gets to the second contact).

## Per-subskill canonical cues

### The Three P's (Munciana defensive identity)

**"Posture, position, passion."** Introduced 12s. Lifelong. Lingenfelter's actual words, spoken while setting up the Seat-to-Seam drill at 00:19:45 of the camp tape: "Seat to seam is teaching posture, position, and then passion. Now, the three P's of defense, they're going to get back. Have a quick seat. They got to tap their tush on the box. That tells me they understand position, that tells me they understand posture, and it also tells me on the second ball that they understand passion because they're going to throw a seam ball" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches the three defensive priorities as one identity phrase, and the drill makes each of them checkable: the tush has to reach the box (position), it can only get there from a loaded low seat (posture), and the seam ball thrown off the second contact is the passion. Say the P's in this order, posture first, because that is the order on the tape. Does NOT teach a three-step sequence; the P's are simultaneous, and the names exist to organize the coaching, not the movement.

**"Get back, get low, get a touch."** Introduced 12s. Lifelong. **The program's teaching gloss on the Three P's, standardized in this word order.** These are not Lingenfelter's words. He says "get back" on the tape while describing the Seat-to-Seam start; "get low" and "get a touch" are the program's own rendering of posture and passion into language a 12-year-old can act on inside a rep [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Use the gloss on the court and the P's when you are teaching the concept, and do not present the gloss as a Munciana quotation. Does NOT teach three separate corrections: it is one phrase said as one phrase.

**"Get back."** Introduced 12s. Lifelong. The position half of the gloss. Teaches the first defensive priority: arrive at the proper defensive starting position before the hitter contacts the ball. A defender who is still moving forward at hitter contact has ceded the dig before the swing happens. Does NOT teach a fixed depth — the back depth varies by formation; the cue installs the *priority* of being back.

**"Get low."** Introduced 12s. Lifelong. The posture half of the gloss. Teaches the loaded athletic posture defenders hold during the read — knees bent, weight forward, hands ready in front. A defender standing tall at hitter contact cannot push off into pursuit. Does NOT teach a fixed posture height — the squat depth varies by player and situation; the cue installs the *priority* of being low.

**"Get a touch."** Introduced 12s. Lifelong. The passion half of the gloss. Teaches that the dig does not have to be perfect, it has to *exist*: a touch on every ball, including an emergency touch, is the unit of defensive success. Does NOT teach pursuing every ball at all costs — the cue installs the *intent* to touch; physical limits remain.

**"Start low, stay low, play low."** Introduced 11s. Lifelong. Lingenfelter's Mountain D framing captured verbatim at 00:17:39: "Mountain D is a drill you will see. It's really about teaching your kids start low, stay low, play low" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches that the low posture is held across the whole rally rather than assumed once and abandoned after the first contact. The drill enforces it by starting players in a mountain-climber position and resetting them there when the ball dies. Does NOT teach a static squat; the players are moving the entire time, they are just moving low.

### Perimeter base (Three-Way Release)

**"Outside foot high."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. The Munciana perimeter-defense stagger captured verbatim from Three-Way Release at 00:14:03: "Make sure everybody's outside foot is high. We want to make sure the outside foot is high every time. So that means the foot on the line needs to be ahead of the foot in the court so their bellies are angled back and in the court. This doesn't happen by accident, it has to be taught" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches the wing defender's staggered base on the perimeter: the sideline foot ("the foot on the line") sits ahead of the court-side foot, which turns the belly back and in toward the middle of the court. From that stagger the defender can release up the line or cross through the middle without a reset step. Lingenfelter runs the drill with the DSs and the outside hitters, so it is a wing-defender cue, not a libero-only one. Does NOT teach a square perimeter base, and does NOT teach anything about blocking: this cue was mis-filed as a pin-blocking cue in earlier revisions of [[blocking-cues]].

**"Cut the outside foot."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. The movement companion, captured at 00:15:06 during the roll-through-the-middle phase: "Outside foot's got to be high. Make sure you cut the outside foot" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches that the release out of the perimeter base starts by cutting the sideline foot across, so the defender's first step is already committed toward the ball rather than spent squaring up. Does NOT teach a crossover for every ball: the cut is the release mechanic from a staggered base; short balls are still played with a shuffle.

**"Seal the perimeter."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. Captured at 00:14:30: "Rolling up the line, they come, they defend arms down. They seal the perimeter, they defend coming up the line" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches the wing defender's job description in one phrase: nothing gets down between her and the sideline, and she arrives with arms already down rather than dropping them on the way. Does NOT teach a line-only responsibility: the perimeter includes the corner and the short line ball; the seal is about the boundary the defender owns, not a single shot.

### Reading

**"Read and run."** Introduced 11s. Lifelong. Munciana's defensive identity phrase, captured at 00:12:29 as Lingenfelter introduces Speedball: "Speedball is the intro to defense. We want people to learn to read and run. Most of the time your guys are watching and standing. So we want to read and run" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches that the read is only half of it: the read has to produce movement on the same beat, and a defender who reads correctly and then watches has not defended. The named failure mode is the useful part of this cue: "watching and standing" is the thing you are correcting, and players recognize it in themselves immediately. Does NOT teach guessing early: the run follows the read; it does not replace it.

**"Look, See, Decide."** Introduced 13s. Refined 15s+. The Josephson three-step reading frame captured directly [aoc-2025-training-reading-look-see-decide]: "'The look' is the teachable technique: where to watch — approach angle, hips, shoulder angle, hand. 'The see' is the intangible interpretation step. ... 'The decide' is the response choice that follows." Teaches the three-stage reading process: where to look (approach angle, hips, shoulder, hand), what to see (the interpretation that names hitter intent), how to decide (the movement choice). Does NOT teach a fast-cognitive process — at high levels Look-See-Decide compresses into a unified perception, but the diagnosis tool stays compartmentalized.

**"What did you see?"** Introduced 13s. Refined 15s+. The Josephson coach-question that drives the See step [aoc-2025-training-reading-look-see-decide]: "coaches probe it by asking 'What did you see?' and then telling players what to see." Teaches the verbal debrief that develops the player's perceptual vocabulary — between reps, the coach asks what the player saw, then names what *should* have been seen. Does NOT teach a single right answer — the question opens the discussion; the coaching follows.

**"Read the hitter, not the ball."** Introduced 13s. Refined 15s+. The classic defensive reading priority. Teaches that the defender's eyes commit to the hitter's hips, shoulder angle, and arm-swing window during the approach — the ball is a distraction at the moment of decision. The Josephson Look frame names the same cues: "approach angle, hips, shoulder angle, hand" [aoc-2025-training-reading-look-see-decide]. Does NOT teach ignoring the ball — the eyes return to the ball at contact, but the hitter's body has already given the read.

**"Ball-setter-ball-hitter."** Introduced 13s. Refined 15s+. The Kiraly B-S-B-H eye-progression applied to defense [aoc-2024-kiraly-reads-every-skill][kiraly-1997-championship-volleyball]. Teaches the same four-step eye sequence used in blocking — see the ball off the pass, find the setter, return to the ball as it leaves the setter's hands, then commit eyes to the hitter at the apex. Does NOT teach staring at the hitter — eyes track through the four steps, ending on the hitter.

**"What do I see? What do I know? How do I respond?"** Introduced 15s. Refined 17s+. The Kiraly three-question frame applied to defense [aoc-2024-kiraly-reads-every-skill]. Teaches the perception-knowledge-response chain that separates reading-as-skill from reading-as-instinct. At the advanced level this is a debrief tool more than a real-time cue.

### Posture & platform

**"Hips under the ball."** Introduced 13s. Refined 15s+. The Stone digging-position cue captured directly from the AOC source [aoc-2025-controlling-the-dig]: "Get hips under the ball so the torso can remain upright on hard-driven attacks." Teaches that on a hard-driven dig the defender drops her hips low enough that the torso stays upright, allowing the platform to manage trajectory rather than sending the dig flat or backward. Stone's framing: "An upright torso lets the defender manage both trajectory and platform angle" [aoc-2025-controlling-the-dig]. Does NOT teach a permanent low position — the hips drop to meet the ball; the cue is for the moment of contact.

**"Drop to the knees if you have to."** Introduced 14s. Refined 16s+. The Stone advanced-defensive-posture cue [aoc-2025-controlling-the-dig]: "Dropping to the knees is a legitimate advanced defensive posture, not a failure mode." Teaches that the kneel-dig position is a real defensive tool, not an emergency. Stone's example is Morgan Hentz dropping to her knees to control a hard attack [aoc-2025-controlling-the-dig]. Does NOT teach defaulting to kneel-digs — the cue installs the *legitimacy* of the move, not its frequency.

**"Late platform."** Introduced 13s. Lifelong. Teaches that the platform forms in the last half-second before contact, not on the way to the ball. Same correction as in passing — pre-formed platforms cannot adjust to a hit that drops or drifts. Does NOT teach forming the platform *at* contact — the platform should be ready before contact, just not built early.

**"Quiet platform."** Introduced 13s. Lifelong. Borrowed from the passing cue dictionary, applied to defense. Teaches that the platform does not swing through the ball — it presents a stationary surface, and the body provides what little redirection is needed. The dig that "controls" — Stone's framing — comes from a quiet platform that doesn't push the ball into the rafters [aoc-2025-controlling-the-dig].

### Pursuit

**"Move your feet, not your arms."** Introduced 11s. Lifelong. Same cue as in passing. Teaches that the legs move the body to the ball, not the platform. The single most repeated correction in defensive teaching. Does NOT teach a perfect-feet rule — emergency moves are real (see below).

**"Arrive stopped, then platform."** Introduced 13s. Refined 15s+. Teaches the two-step ideal: pursuit footwork first, stationary plant second, platform formation third. A defender who is still moving at contact produces an off-angle dig. Does NOT teach a stopped-only rule — at advanced levels, run-throughs and one-arm digs are part of the inventory; the cue is the *ideal*, not the only path.

**"Stopped and ready to react."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. The Munciana physio-reaction standard captured at 00:16:39: "We want to make sure our players are stopped and ready to react" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Lingenfelter names it as the thing he is checking as a coach on every rep of that drill: "as a coach, I just make sure they're released. I make sure they're stopped" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches the stop that has to happen before the reaction. A defender still drifting when the ball leaves the hitter's hand reacts late in whichever direction she was already going. Pairs with "Arrive stopped, then platform" below; this one is about the moment *before* the attack, that one is about the moment before contact. Does NOT teach standing still: the stop is a loaded, momentary plant inside continuous movement.

**"Cross, don't stand and look."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. The Figure-Eight Defense cue captured at 00:22:12: "It's a teacher, guys, to move on defense. We want defenders to learn to cross, not stand and look" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches that a ball outside the defender's immediate reach is answered with a crossover step that covers ground, rather than with a lean and a watch. The figure-eight pattern (short ball, then middle, then long, always in the direction the defender just came from) exists to force the crossover under fatigue. Does NOT teach a crossover for every ball: one-step balls stay on the shuffle; the cue targets the two-plus-step ball where players default to reaching.

**"Shuffle, don't reach."** Introduced 11s. Lifelong. The defensive-pursuit footwork cue. Teaches that lateral movement uses shuffle steps for one-step balls (and crossover for two-plus-step balls), with the platform formation deferred until arrival. A defender who reaches with the platform across her body produces a flat dig. Does NOT teach a shuffle-only rule — the inventory includes shuffle, crossover, drop-step, and run-through; the cue installs the *priority* of footwork over arm extension.

### Emergency moves

**"Center to the ball, attack the attacker."** Introduced 14s. Refined 16s+. The Flynn-Oldenburg emergency-defensive cue captured directly [aoc-2022-emergency-moves-passing-defense]: "Emergency moves work from 'GO posture,' face the action, and get belly button (center) to and under the ball; 'attack the attacker.'" Teaches that emergency moves face the attack and aggressively close center-to-ball, rather than reaching from a passive stance. Does NOT teach charging at the hitter — the cue installs the *direction* of emergency movement (forward into the attack), not a literal charge.

**"Double Knee Down."** Introduced 14s. Refined 16s+. The Flynn-Oldenburg specific emergency-move name [aoc-2022-emergency-moves-passing-defense]: "Double Knee Down: controlled move to block the ball with body when a hard-driven ball lands in front of defender below the waist." Teaches a named emergency posture: knees drop simultaneously, body absorbs the ball with platform high. Does NOT teach a slide — the Double Knee Down is stationary; the slide variant is the Shin Dig.

**"Shin Dig."** Introduced 14s. Refined 16s+. The Flynn-Oldenburg companion emergency move [aoc-2022-emergency-moves-passing-defense]: "Shin Dig: one shin slides, other knee touches down, finishes in Double Knee Down — used for balls aggressively attacked left or right but not far enough for a traditional floor move." Teaches the lateral emergency move that ends in the Double Knee Down position. Flynn-Oldenburg's analogy: "a catcher blocking a wild pitch, but the defender attacks the ball" [aoc-2022-emergency-moves-passing-defense]. Does NOT teach a roll — the Shin Dig stays vertical at the end.

**"One Knee Down."** Introduced 13s. Refined 15s+. The Flynn-Oldenburg passing-and-defense low-ball cue [aoc-2022-emergency-moves-passing-defense]: "Passing's One Knee Down creates a solid foundation for getting center under a low ball." Teaches a stable low platform on a low ball — one knee drops, platform stays high, dig is controlled rather than punched. Does NOT teach a kneeling default — the cue installs the move, not a frequency.

### Recovery & cover

**"Up after the dig."** Introduced 12s. Refined 14s+. Teaches the immediate post-dig motion: the defender returns to her feet ready for the next contact. A defender who stays low after the dig surrenders coverage. Does NOT teach abandoning the dig — the upright recovery is the *next* motion, not the dig itself.

**"Hands on the floor before the ball is caught."** Introduced 13s. Lifelong. Munciana's coverage standard, and the reason coverage is checkable rather than aspirational. Lingenfelter, coaching Five-and-Five setting at 00:34:10 to 00:34:19: "The cover guy wants to be there by the time the ball is caught. Otherwise you're not setting high enough. Set it high. Get there before the ball is caught. ... When we cover, we put our hands on the floor to signify we're there. If the hands are late, that means we didn't get where we need to go" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches a coverage deadline with a visible pass/fail: the coverer's hands are down on the floor before the out-of-system set is caught. Late hands are not a coverage style, they are a diagnosis that the player did not arrive. The second half of the quote is the coach's half: if nobody can make the deadline, the set was too low, and the correction lands on the setter rather than the coverers.

**"Nobody from the hitting team is last back inside the 10."** Introduced 14s. Refined 16s+. The team-level coverage audit from the Net Six game at 00:58:47: "the team that is hitting should never be the last guys back in front of the 10, because we should be in coverage. So as a coach, it's one way to figure out whether you're covering or not" [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. Teaches the whole-team version of the same standard, and gives the staff a scoreable in-rally test that needs no video: if your attacking side is the last to get everyone inside the 10-foot line, your coverage did not happen. Does NOT teach crowding the hitter: the test measures arrival, not proximity.

**"Cover the hitter."** Introduced 13s. Lifelong. The umbrella phrase for the responsibility the two cues above make measurable: once the team transitions to attack, the non-hitting players close in to play the block-touch return. Coverage geometry varies by attack tempo and rotation, so the phrase installs the responsibility and the two Munciana standards install the check. Does NOT teach a fixed cover position.

## Anti-cues

- **"Just dig it up."** Vague outcome cue. Modern correction: "Get a touch" — the touch is the unit, framed as a process win.
- **"Watch the ball."** Wrong cue priority. Modern correction: "Read the hitter, not the ball."
- **"Stand tall."** Wrong defensive posture. Modern correction: "Get low" — the loaded posture is the defender's default.
- **"Reach with your platform."** Encourages arm extension instead of footwork. Modern correction: "Move your feet, not your arms."
- **"Pre-form the platform."** The legacy passing-style cue applied incorrectly to defense. Modern correction: "Late platform" — the platform forms at the last moment to match the dig angle.
- **"Bend your knees more."** Internally-focused leg-position cue. Modern correction: "Get low" or "Hips under the ball" — externalizes attention onto the relationship.
- **"Don't let it hit the floor."** Negative outcome cue. Modern correction: "Get a touch" — same intent, framed as process.
- **"Watch the hitter's eyes."** Wrong cue target — the hitter's eyes are not a reliable read source. Modern correction: "Look at the hips and shoulders" per Josephson's Look frame [aoc-2025-training-reading-look-see-decide].
- **"Catch it on your chest."** Wrong dig technique for hard-driven balls. Modern correction: "Hips under the ball" plus "Late platform" — the platform makes the contact, not the chest.
- **"Don't dive unless you have to."** Discourages emergency moves and produces hesitant pursuit. Modern correction: name the emergency moves ("Double Knee Down," "Shin Dig," roll) so they are real tools, not last-resort gambles.
- **"Cover if you can get there."** Makes coverage optional and unmeasurable. Modern correction: "Hands on the floor before the ball is caught," a deadline with a visible pass/fail [munciana-2022-camp-drills].
- **"See where it goes, then move."** Splits the read from the movement and produces exactly the watching-and-standing habit Lingenfelter names. Modern correction: "Read and run" [munciana-2022-camp-drills].

## Voice consistency notes

The defense cue vocabulary is genuinely contested across schools, and the dictionary preserves the disagreement.

**Modern AOC** (Stone, Josephson, Liskevych, Flynn-Oldenburg, Kiraly) leads the program's working voice on reading, posture, platform management, and emergency moves [aoc-2025-controlling-the-dig][aoc-2025-training-reading-look-see-decide][aoc-2017-liskevych-asics-defense][aoc-2022-emergency-moves-passing-defense][aoc-2024-kiraly-reads-every-skill]. Hips under the ball, Look/See/Decide, Ball-Setter-Ball-Hitter, and the named emergency-move inventory are all canonical AOC vocabulary.

**Munciana** owns the largest share of this dictionary [munciana-2022-camp-drills]. The Three P's are Lingenfelter's words in his order (posture, position, passion, at 00:19:45 of the camp tape); "get back, get low, get a touch" is the program's own gloss on them and should be introduced as such rather than quoted back as Munciana language. Beyond the P's, the camp library supplies the perimeter-base stagger from Three-Way Release, the read-and-run identity framing from Speedball, the stopped-and-ready standard from the physio-reaction drill, the cross-don't-stand-and-look correction from Figure-Eight, and the two coverage standards (hands on the floor before the ball is caught; the hitting team is never last back inside the 10). What makes the Munciana material distinctive is not the vocabulary so much as the checkability: nearly every cue on the tape comes attached to something the coach can see happen or fail to happen on the rep.

**Japanese tradition** carries the strongest claim on defensive identity through the dig-up-everything ethic, the midline-the-ball priority, and the recover-to-ready-after-every-contact culture. The Munciana Three P's converge with the Japanese frame at the *intent* level — both traditions emphasize that defense is a culture, not a technique. The cue vocabulary differs (Munciana's three-P's vs. Japanese kaiten/midline language), but the priorities align. The program leans Munciana phrasing because it's the working-club voice, but the Japanese tradition's defensive-culture argument is preserved on the [[defense]] hub.

**GMS** argues defense emerges from random/variable practice at game-like intensity rather than from decoupled reading drills. The cue language in this dictionary is compatible — Look/See/Decide, hips under the ball, and the emergency-move names all work in any practice architecture. GMS's distinct contribution is the *practice-design* argument that reading drills should be embedded in live-hitter feeds, which is procedural rather than lexical.

**Ecological dynamics** prefers cues that constrain the task rather than instruct movement. Look/See/Decide, "What did you see?", and "Read the hitter, not the ball" are all task-relational cues that work in eco-dynamics-style constraints-led practice. The dictionary's vocabulary leans this way per the recency preference.

When schools genuinely disagree on a defense cue — for example, "watch the ball off the hitter's hand" (legacy ball-tracking cue) versus "read the hitter's hips and shoulders" (modern reading cue) — the dictionary keeps both with attribution. The program's working voice is the modern reading frame.

The phrases every coach in the program should say: get back, get low, get a touch (the gloss on posture, position, passion), start low stay low play low, read and run, Look/See/Decide, what did you see, read the hitter not the ball, hips under the ball, late platform, quiet platform, move your feet not your arms, stopped and ready to react, arrive stopped then platform, shuffle don't reach, cross don't stand and look, outside foot high, cut the outside foot, seal the perimeter, center to the ball, up after the dig, hands on the floor before the ball is caught. Twenty-one phrases. Repeat them.
